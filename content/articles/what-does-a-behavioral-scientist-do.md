A behavioral scientist studies why people do what they do, designs changes that make a target behavior easier, and tests whether those changes worked. The behavior might be paying a tax bill on time, finishing a sign-up or opening a social app less often, and the job is the same each time: find what stops it, change the conditions around it, and measure what people actually do afterwards.

I'm a behavioral scientist in Milan, working on websites, funnels, apps and games. Below: where behavioral scientists work, how a project runs, the frameworks, skills and evidence, and how the role differs from its neighbors.

## Where do behavioral scientists work?

Government made the job visible. The UK's Behavioural Insights Team, the "Nudge Unit" set up in 2010 in No 10 and the Cabinet Office, was the first government team of its kind. Many governments followed: the OECD's 2024 LOGIC report says its online map "now records over 200 government teams applying behavioural science in 58 countries."

Companies are a large part of the picture too. In a 2020 international survey of applied teams by Stephen Wendel, with the Behavioral Science Policy Association and the Action Design Network, 65% of the organizations whose type was known were for-profit companies. The median team had four people, more than half of the companies and nonprofits were consultancies, and in-house teams sat most often in data science, product, marketing and design. The survey was voluntary and skewed toward the US, the UK and the Netherlands.

Others work in nonprofits and universities. Outside academia, most are applied behavioral scientists: the output is a change in behavior, not a paper. Searching for behavioral science jobs? Look beyond the exact title, to roles such as behavior scientist, behavioral designer or researcher in data science, product, marketing and design teams.

## What does a behavioral science project look like?

Most projects follow some version of one loop, which I call the four-step behavior loop. Only what survives step four gets scaled:

1. **Define one behavior.** Who does what, by when, measured how. "Engagement" is not a behavior; "new customers place a second order within 30 days" is.
2. **Diagnose the barriers.** Data shows where people stop; interviews and observation show why.
3. **Design for the confirmed barrier.** Choose the smallest change that removes it: a default, a removed step, better timing, clearer feedback.
4. **Test before you scale.** Randomize where you can, and decide the outcome, the comparison and the smallest effect worth acting on before any data arrives.

Here is the loop on a hypothetical project: a budgeting app where most new users connect a bank account and never set a budget.

- **Behavior:** a new user sets a first budget within 48 hours of connecting an account.
- **Diagnosis:** people glance at their spending and leave. Interviews suggest they don't know what limits to choose (capability), the budget screen sits three taps from where sessions end (opportunity), and the payoff feels distant (motivation).
- **Design:** right after the account connects, offer a budget pre-filled from last month's spending, ready to accept or edit. Defaults change what happens when people do nothing, as organ-donation consent showed (Johnson & Goldstein, 2003).
- **Test:** randomize new users between the old flow and the new one. Primary measure: a budget set at 48 hours. Secondary: still in use at day 30, because a default people accept and then ignore isn't a behavior change.

If the day-30 number doesn't move, go back to diagnosis, not to a louder version of the same idea.

## Which frameworks do behavioral scientists use?

Three come up constantly. The last column is my reading of what each is best at:

| Framework | What it says | Most useful for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| COM-B (Michie, van Stralen & West, 2011) | Behavior arises from capability, opportunity and motivation, which interact | Diagnosing why a behavior isn't happening |
| EAST (Behavioural Insights Team, 2014) | Make it Easy, Attractive, Social and Timely | A quick check on a design |
| MINDSPACE (Dolan and colleagues, 2010) | Nine influences: messenger, incentives, norms, defaults, salience, priming, affect, commitments, ego | Auditing the influences already at work in a policy or product |

COM-B sits at the hub of the Behaviour Change Wheel, and each component splits in two: capability is physical or psychological, opportunity is physical or social, and motivation is reflective (plans, evaluations) or automatic (emotions, impulses, habits). Product teams also use Fogg's (2009) model, in which behavior needs motivation, ability and a prompt at the same moment.

My view: frameworks help you ask better questions, but they don't replace research. A COM-B diagnosis without data is a guess with labels on it.

## What skills does a behavioral scientist need?

My short list:

- **Defining behavior precisely**, because a vague target makes every later step vague.
- **Research design and statistics:** experiments, sample sizes, repeated measures and regression, and the judgment to match a design to a question.
- **Qualitative research:** interviews and observation, coded systematically, for example with thematic analysis (Braun & Clarke, 2006).
- **Knowing the evidence and its limits:** which effects hold up, and which shrink outside the lab.
- **Translation:** writing a result so a designer, an engineer or a minister can act on it.
- **Ethics:** techniques that make a good behavior easier can make a bad one easier too. Brignull (2010) coined "dark patterns" for interfaces that trick people.

If you're applying for these roles, show one behavior you defined, one test you designed and what you did when the result came back flat. In my opinion that says more than a list of biases.

## Do nudges work as well as the books suggest?

Usually less well, and a good behavioral scientist plans for that. DellaVigna and Linos (2022) compared trials run by nudge units with nudge studies published in academic journals, and the nudge-unit effects were much smaller. A large meta-analysis of choice architecture found an average effect (Mertens et al., 2022), but a reanalysis that adjusted for publication bias found little evidence left (Maier et al., 2022).

That doesn't make the field useless; it changes the job: test several candidate changes against each other and a control, as the megastudy by Milkman and colleagues (2021) did, and expect small effects. I go through the evidence in [whether nudges actually work](/insights/do-nudges-work-evidence-for-product-teams).

## How is a behavioral scientist different from a psychologist, an economist or a data scientist?

The overlap is large; the clearest difference is the starting question:

| Role | Starting question | Typical output |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Behavioral scientist | What stops this behavior, and what would make it easier? | A diagnosis, a designed change, a test result |
| Psychologist | How do mind and behavior work, and how can a person be helped? | Research, or assessment and support for individuals |
| Economist | How do incentives, prices and markets shape choices? | Models, forecasts, policy analysis |
| Data scientist | What patterns are in the data, and what comes next? | Predictive models, analyses, dashboards |

There is also a legal difference. In Italy, only psychologists enrolled in Section A of the Albo of the Ordine degli Psicologi may use the title "psicologo" or practice the profession (L. 56/1989, art. 2; DPR 328/2001, art. 50). I trained in experimental psychology, but I'm not registered, so I don't use the title: I work as a behavioral scientist and consumer psychology consultant, on decisions and products, not therapy.

I compare the options in [which behavior expert you need](/insights/behavior-expert-behavioral-scientist-consumer-psychologist). This is general information, not legal advice, and rules change, so check with the national professional body.

## What does this look like in my own work?

My projects sit at different points on the loop:

- **A controlled test.** My M.Sc. thesis was an experiment on automated interviews: each of 96 participants answered in three formats, self-view, an AI avatar and text only. A repeated-measures ANOVA with Holm-corrected post hoc tests showed that candidates were significantly more expressive with the AI avatar than when they saw themselves, p = .009. The [interview simulation study](/work/interview-simulation-study) page has the design, and [within-subjects UX experiments](/insights/within-subjects-ux-experiments) explains the method.
- **A design built on a diagnosis.** [FloraLock, ShroomLock and CloudLock](/work/app-lockers) put a question in front of Instagram or TikTok: to open the app, you name the plant, mushroom or cloud in a photo. Blockers that only say no tend to get switched off, so this friction teaches something, and escape hatches let people through rather than drive them to uninstall.
- **A test that is still a plan.** Costly, my pre-launch commitment app, charges real money for every minute of doomscrolling. It has no users and no results yet, only a planned randomized three-arm study, awareness vs soft friction vs real stakes, so the stakes must beat the cheaper options to earn their place.
- **A borrowed behavior.** On Passway, a team project with engineer friends, students weren't entering enough data about their courses. I suggested the swipe from dating apps, changed to fit data entry, and in a test with engineering students they entered much more data and enjoyed it more.

If you're wondering whether your problem needs a behavioral scientist, write the behavior you want in one sentence, with a who, a what and a when. If you can't, that's the first piece of work. If you can, the next question is what stops it, and that's where my [behavioral science consulting](/behavioral-science) begins.
