Online consumer psychology studies how people perceive, judge and decide when they shop through a screen, and most of its core questions have held up for two decades. How published word of mouth passes product experiences between consumers, whether an ad works when nobody clicks, how a site's look relates to what people think of its products, and how people choose with a shopping agent's help were all on the table in 2005, and each still matters for a website, an app or a funnel.

What changed is the setting: phones instead of desktops, feeds instead of homepages, AI assistants where there were shopping agents. To separate what holds from what doesn't, I use one book as a time capsule: "Online Consumer Psychology: Understanding and Influencing Consumer Behavior in the Virtual World", edited by Haugtvedt, Machleit and Yalch (2005). The verdicts below are my opinion, from designing websites, funnels and apps, not claims the chapters make.

## What is the book "Online Consumer Psychology" about?

It's an edited volume with an introduction and 23 chapters, published in 2005 by Lawrence Erlbaum Associates and now sold by Psychology Press. The editors were Haugtvedt (Ohio State University), Machleit (University of Cincinnati) and Yalch (University of Washington). It contains edited versions of papers presented at the 2001 Advertising and Consumer Psychology Conference in Seattle, sponsored by the Society for Consumer Psychology. It builds on an earlier conference volume, "Advertising and the World Wide Web" (Schumann & Thorson, 1999).

Its six parts show what consumer behavior in the virtual world meant to researchers then:

1. Community (4 chapters), including ritual behavior in online consumption communities (Toder-Alon, Brunel & Schneier Siegal) and "Published Word of Mouth", on reviews and forums (Schindler & Bickart).
2. Advertising (4), including how banner ads affect brand choice without click-through (Mitchell & Valenzuela), what affects click-through rate (Chandon & Chtourou) and consumer response to "advergaming" (Nelson).
3. Customization (4), including preference construction in agent-assisted online shopping (Murray & Häubl).
4. Site Design (3), including the effects of visual consistency on site identity and product attitude (Omanson, Cline & Nordhielm).
5. Decision Making (6), including how to combine online and offline shopping features (Levin, Levin & Heath) and an exploratory study of online auctions (Sivadas, Stern, Mehta & Jones).
6. Research Tools and Approaches (2), including web-based consumer research (Englis, Solomon & Danskin).

Two topics I'd expect today are absent: there's no chapter on online trust and none on web atmospherics, the "store atmosphere" of a page. For atmospherics, the closest is the Site Design part.

## Which ideas from 2005 still hold?

Four of the book's questions still apply almost unchanged, in my view.

**Published word of mouth.** Schindler and Bickart's chapter on online reviews and forums frames them as "published word of mouth": consumer-generated information that others can refer to. That holds today; what changed is the scale. Uncertain people look to what others like them have done (Cialdini, 1984), and online they can read it at the moment of decision.

**Ads without clicks.** Mitchell and Valenzuela asked how banner ads affect brand choice without click-through. It's still the right question for feeds, where most people scroll past. A click-through rate captures one behavior, not an ad's whole effect, so I'd judge ads by what people do later.

**The look of a site.** Omanson, Cline and Nordhielm studied the effects of visual consistency on site identity and product attitude. I work on a similar assumption: how a site looks colors how people judge what it sells. So I keep one promise, one look and one call to action consistent across pages and languages.

**Online plus offline.** Levin, Levin and Heath looked for the best ways to combine online and offline shopping features. For considered purchases that mix is now normal: a website is one step in a journey that may end in a call or a meeting, so the next offline step should be visible. The basics of how people decide still apply too; I cover them in [what consumer behavior is](/insights/what-is-consumer-behavior).

## How has online consumer behavior changed since 2005?

The changes I notice most are about where decisions happen, not how minds work.

- **Phones and feeds.** The papers date from 2001, when shopping online mostly meant sitting at a computer. Today a first visit often starts on a phone, mid-scroll, on whatever page a link points to, so the first screen has to state the offer before the thumb moves on.
- **Social proof at scale.** Reviews sit almost everywhere, and people know they can be gamed. The question has moved from "do we have reviews?" to "can anyone check them?"
- **Value before commitment.** In my experience, people now expect to get something before they hand over an email address or create an account.
- **A name for the dark side.** Brignull coined "dark patterns" in 2010, after the book, and Mathur and colleagues (2019) examined about 11,000 shopping websites and found dark patterns in use at scale.

## How do AI assistants change online consumer psychology?

The biggest update is to the Customization part, where Murray and Häubl studied preference construction in agent-assisted online shopping. In 2005 the agent was a shopping tool; today it can be an AI assistant that searches, compares, summarizes and sometimes acts for the shopper. If preferences are partly built during the choice, as the chapter's framing implies, an assistant isn't just answering a question but helping to form the answer.

That moves two trust problems into shopping. Lee and See (2004) argued that trust in automation should match what the system can actually do, and Dietvorst, Simmons and Massey (2015) found that people lose confidence in an algorithm quickly after seeing it err. My design rules follow from both, and they're opinions: show the criteria the assistant used and let people change them, keep a way to check its work against the source, and make clear who makes the final choice.

There's also a new reader. If an assistant summarizes your page for a buyer, that summary may be your first impression, so state the facts that answer a buyer's doubts plainly, where any reader, human or software, can find them.

## Still true, needs updating, new: the then-and-now table

I call this summary the then-and-now table; the verdicts are mine.

| Theme | Verdict | Design implication today |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Published word of mouth (Community) | Still true, at far larger scale | Put specific, checkable reviews next to the ask |
| Ads without click-through (Advertising) | Still the right question | Judge ads by later behavior, not click-through alone |
| Visual consistency and site identity (Site Design) | Still relevant, now on a phone | One promise on the first phone screen, one look everywhere |
| Agent-assisted shopping (Customization) | Needs updating: the agent is now an AI assistant | Show the assistant's criteria, let people edit them, keep a way to check |
| Online consumption communities (Community) | Needs updating: they live on others' platforms | Earn reputation where people talk, and answer there |
| Web-based consumer research (Research Tools) | Still useful, with a warning | Pair analytics for where people drop with interviews for why |
| Trust in an unknown seller | No chapter in 2005; I'd add it | Place proof and risk reversal where the doubt appears |
| Value before sign-up | Now expected, in my experience | Show a first result before asking for an account |
| Dark patterns | New since 2005: named in 2010 | Test every persuasive element for honesty before it ships |

The warning in the research row comes from Pham (2013), who argued that cheap online data can steer what gets studied. I go through his critique in [the seven sins of consumer psychology](/insights/seven-sins-of-consumer-psychology-pham).

[Optimolds](/work/optimolds), an Italy-based composite engineering and mold-making firm, sells to risk-averse buyers: a bad mold ruins a production run. On the trilingual website and lead-generation funnel I designed and built, trust signals (reviews, reply time, accepted CAD formats, based in Italy) sit next to one clear call to action: a free 48-hour feasibility check that takes the risk out of the first step. The form is a short CAD upload. That's the trust row in practice; I explain the placement in [trust signals for a brand nobody knows yet](/insights/trust-signals-unknown-brand).

[OopsCupid](/work/oopscupid), a relationship-assessment platform I designed and launched, takes the value-before-sign-up row literally. Its 15 free research-informed tests ask for no sign-up before the first result, a deliberate trade of email capture for a shorter path. Responses are scored by fixed rules into a six-axis profile, and an LLM only writes the narrative readout, which keeps each profile consistent and checkable.
