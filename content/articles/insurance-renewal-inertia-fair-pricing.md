Renewal inertia is the tendency of policyholders to let a policy roll over without comparing price or cover, because sticking with the status quo is effortless and switching feels like work and risk. Inertia can also be priced in. The practice is called price walking: loyal customers gradually paying more than new ones for the same cover. In the UK, the FCA stopped it for home and motor insurance from 1 January 2022.

I've never worked for an insurer, and this is not an inside account of renewal pricing. It's a consumer psychology consultant's reading of why inertia is so strong, what the UK rules changed, and what I think product teams anywhere should take from it.

## What is status quo bias, and why is insurance so exposed to it?

Samuelson and Zeckhauser (1988) showed that people disproportionately stick with the status quo, and not only in the lab: their data on real choices of health plans and retirement programs showed the same pull in decisions that mattered. Insurance renewal is almost a laboratory for it. The current policy is the default, the alternatives are many and hard to compare, and the renewal arrives by itself.

Defaults are powerful everywhere. Madrian and Shea (2001) found that automatic enrollment sharply raised participation in retirement saving and traced much of the effect to inertia, and Johnson and Goldstein (2003) showed the same force in organ donation. Insurance has its own classic case: Johnson, Hershey, Meszaros and Kunreuther (1993) describe two neighboring US states that made different car-insurance options the default, and most drivers kept whichever one their state had chosen.

## Why do customers stay even when they're paying more?

Three forces stack up, and only one of them looks like laziness.

- **Effort.** Comparing quotes means re-entering details, decoding cover and making a decision nobody enjoys. Fogg (2009) would call it an ability problem: the switching behavior is simply hard.
- **Fear of a worse loss.** Losses loom larger than gains (Kahneman & Tversky, 1979). Saving money by switching is a gain; ending up with an insurer that doesn't pay is a loss, and it weighs more.
- **The known versus the unknown.** People prefer known odds to unknown ones (Ellsberg, 1961). The current insurer is a known quantity; a new one is ambiguous, however good its price looks.

None of this is irrational from the inside. That is exactly why profiting from it feels unfair from the outside.

## What did the UK ban on price walking change?

The FCA's general insurance pricing practices rules, PS21/5, came into force on 1 January 2022. For home and motor insurance, a renewal price can be no higher than the equivalent new-business price through the same channel. And stopping an auto-renewal must be as easy as setting it up.

The first rule removes the payoff from inertia: a customer who doesn't shop around should not pay a loyalty penalty for it. The second rule targets the asymmetry that makes inertia sticky in the first place. If joining takes one click and leaving takes a phone call, the default does the selling.

These rules apply in the UK, not the EU. Still, they are a clear statement of where one regulator drew the line, and I'd expect customers everywhere to hold insurers to a similar standard of fairness. This is not legal advice.

## The opt-in test

Here is the question I'd put to any renewal team, wherever it operates: if renewal required an active yes, would most of your customers still renew?

If the answer is yes, your retention is built on value, and you have little to fear from making renewal easier to stop. If the answer is no, part of your book is held by inertia, and its economics depend on customers not looking. That is fragile, commercially and reputationally.

You can estimate the answer without changing anything. Compare customers who actively confirmed their renewal with those who let it roll over: in claims, complaints and the following year's retention. Large differences between the two groups tell you which kind of retention you have.

## The Fair Renewal Test

Before a renewal notice goes out, I'd check it against five questions:

1. **Same price as new?** Would a new customer with the same risk pay less through the same channel? If so, can you defend why?
2. **Comparison visible?** Does the notice show last year's price next to this year's, with the main reason for any change?
3. **Value shown?** Does it say what the policy did this year, such as claims handled or cover used, and not only what it costs?
4. **Exit symmetric?** Can the customer stop the renewal as easily as they started the policy?
5. **Review invited?** Does it ask whether anything changed, like a new car, a move or a new child, that should change the cover?

Usage-based policies have an advantage on the third question, because they can show customers what actually improved over the year; I look at that in [telematics insurance and behavior change](/insights/telematics-insurance-behavior-change).

## Anatomy of a fair renewal notice

The same hypothetical home insurance notice, written two ways:

| Element | Inertia version | Fair version |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Subject line | "Your policy will renew automatically" | "Your home insurance renewal: your price and what changed" |
| Price | New premium, somewhere in the body text | Last year's and this year's price side by side, with the main reason for the change |
| Value | Nothing | What the policy covered this year, and how to claim |
| Cover check | Small print | Three questions: anything new, anything moved, anything you no longer own? |
| Exit | "Call us to discuss your options" | "Don't want to renew? Stop it here", in one step, in the same channel |

## What my own products taught me about exits

I design my own products on the principle that the exit has to exist and has to be honest. In [Costly](/work/costly), my commitment app, leaving costs a deletion fee, but the user sets that fee in advance, while calm, so the exit is priced openly instead of hidden. In [DeBubble](/work/debubble), my self-care app, progress only goes up and there are no streaks to lose, because I'd rather people come back for the value than stay out of fear of losing something.

Renewals face the same choice at a larger scale: keep customers because leaving is hard, or because staying is worth it. Before shipping any renewal flow, I'd run it through [my five-question test for dark patterns](/insights/persuasion-or-dark-pattern-test).

If you own a renewal journey, run the opt-in test on your last renewal cohort and read the answer honestly. Then rewrite one notice using the fair version above, test it against the current one, and measure retention among customers who actively chose to stay. Retention that survives a clear price comparison and a one-step exit is retention you have earned. The rest is inertia, and inertia is a weak foundation for a customer relationship, especially for a product people already find hard to value, as I explain in [why people under-insure](/insights/why-people-underinsure-psychology).
