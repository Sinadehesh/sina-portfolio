Consumer psychology is the study of how people think, feel and decide as consumers: what they notice, what they want, how they choose, and how they use what they buy. The APA Dictionary of Psychology defines it as "the branch of psychology that studies the behavior of individuals as consumers and the marketing and communication techniques used to influence consumer decisions."

That definition has two halves: the consumer's mind, and the persuasion aimed at it. The second half is why the field has argued about its own ethics for most of its history.

I'm a behavioral scientist and consumer psychology consultant in Milan, and this field is the base of my work. Below is the field in brief, with a checklist for any purchase.

## How do researchers define consumer psychology?

The APA definition continues: "Consumer psychologists investigate the psychological processes underlying consumer behavior and responses to both for-profit and not-for-profit marketing." So the meaning of consumer psychology is wider than shopping: a charity appeal or a public health campaign is as much in scope as a product launch.

The APA Handbook of Consumer Psychology shifts the weight, describing a field that "studies the human behavior of purchase and consumption activities, as well as decisions about products, services, ideas, and experiences" (Kahle, Lowrey & Huber, 2022). The Society for Consumer Psychology is the most concise: "Consumer psychology employs theoretical psychological approaches to understanding consumers."

My own working definition is plainer: consumer psychology explains why people make, or avoid, a choice in a market, using psychology's theories and its methods for testing them. The testing is what separates it from opinions about customers.

## Where did consumer psychology come from?

By 1895 a psychologist was already surveying advertisers. What followed, in brief:

| When | Who | What happened |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1895, 1900 | Harlow Gale, University of Minnesota | Sent advertisers a questionnaire, then published "On the Psychology of Advertising" |
| 1903, 1908 | Walter Dill Scott, Northwestern University | Published "The Theory of Advertising" and "The Psychology of Advertising" |
| 1920–1924 | John B. Watson | Left Johns Hopkins in 1920, joined the J. Walter Thompson agency, became a vice-president in 1924 |
| 1946 | Ernest Dichter | Founded the consulting firm that became the Institute for Motivational Research |
| 1957 | Vance Packard | Published "The Hidden Persuaders", a bestseller that drew negative attention to Dichter and motivational research |
| Early 1960s | Society for Consumer Psychology | Founded as Division 23 of the American Psychological Association; independently incorporated since 1988 |
| 1974, 1992 | Two journals | First issues of the Journal of Consumer Research (1974) and the society's Journal of Consumer Psychology (1992) |

Two things stand out to me. The field's first subject was advertising, not shopping or use. And its ethics were contested early: Packard's book focused criticism on Dichter, a Vienna-born psychologist described as the "originator and leading exponent of motivational research".

The field still lives close to marketing: "the vast majority of academic consumer psychologists work in business schools rather than in social science departments" (Pham, 2013). I describe the jobs in [what a consumer psychologist does](/insights/what-does-a-consumer-psychologist-do).

## What does consumer psychology study?

The topics are easier to use as questions about a purchase, in roughly the order a decision unfolds. I call this my purchase checklist:

| Question | Area of research | Everyday example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1. What did they notice, and what did they miss? | Attention and perception | The reassurance under the button that nobody reads because it's small and gray |
| 2. What came to mind when the need appeared? | Memory | The one brand someone can name when the washing machine breaks |
| 3. What progress are they trying to make? | Motivation | Shoes bought to feel like a runner, not only to run: the "job to be done" (Christensen et al., 2016) |
| 4. How do they feel before, during and after? | Emotion | The "pain of paying", which makes some ways of paying hurt more than others (Prelec & Loewenstein, 1998) |
| 5. What do they believe, and what would change their mind? | Attitudes and persuasion | A buyer who discounts every claim but trusts a demo |
| 6. How do they weigh options, costs and risks? | Judgment and decision-making | A possible loss weighing more than an equal gain (Kahneman & Tversky, 1979) |
| 7. Who else is in the decision? | Social influence | Reviews and "people like you chose this" as social proof (Cialdini, 1984) |
| 8. Do they use it, keep it, recommend it or get rid of it? | Use and after the purchase | The subscription paid every month and never opened |

The last row is the one academic research most often skips. Pham (2013) argued that the field concentrates on the moment of acquisition and its proxies, such as attitudes, choice and willingness to pay, and neglects desire, actual use and disposal. In my experience product teams share the blind spot: they study the comparison in row 6 and miss that people never noticed the offer, or never came back.

## How do consumer psychologists study people?

With psychology's usual toolkit, pointed at buying decisions:

- Experiments: change one thing, such as a price display or a default, and compare what people do.
- Surveys and psychometric scales: measure attitudes, intentions and traits.
- Interviews: reasons in people's own words, often coded with thematic analysis (Braun & Clarke, 2006).
- Behavioral data: purchases, analytics and A/B tests show what people actually did.

Meta-analyses, which pool many studies, deserve more attention than they get. The "too much choice" jam study (Iyengar & Lepper, 2000) is one of the field's best-known findings, yet a meta-analysis found the average effect close to zero, with important moderators (Scheibehenne, Greifeneder & Todd, 2010).

Pham (2013) also criticized the field's reliance on students, online workers and imagined scenarios, and called for more field studies with real consumers. The same applies in business: a survey answer about an imagined purchase is a hypothesis until real buyers behave that way.

## How is consumer psychology used? Examples from marketing, products and policy

Mainly in three places:

- Marketing and communication: what to say, to whom, with what proof, and how to present a price, for companies and charities alike.
- Product design: onboarding, sign-up flows, defaults and pricing pages, where small frictions decide whether a first use becomes a habit.
- Public policy: consent to organ donation was much higher in countries where it was the default (Johnson & Goldstein, 2003), and automatic enrollment raised participation in workplace retirement savings (Madrian & Shea, 2001). Thaler and Sunstein (2008) popularized this kind of design as a "nudge".

One caution: effects shrink outside the journals. Trials run by government nudge units found much smaller effects than published academic studies (DellaVigna & Linos, 2022), so treat a famous effect as a hypothesis for your product, not a promise.

## Where does persuasion become manipulation?

Packard's worry now has a modern name: "dark patterns", coined by Brignull (2010) for designs that work against the people using them, and documented by Mathur and colleagues (2019) across about 11,000 shopping websites.

My working line, and it's an opinion: persuasion gives people true, relevant reasons and keeps the choice easy to refuse; manipulation depends on the person not noticing what's being done to them. So I ask one question of any tactic: would it still work if the customer fully understood it? If not, I don't ship it.

The longer version is my [five-question test for dark patterns](/insights/persuasion-or-dark-pattern-test).

## How is consumer psychology different from consumer behavior and behavioral economics?

The three overlap so much that one study could appear under any of the names. I separate them by the starting question:

- Consumer behavior asks what people do across the whole arc. A definition Pham (2013) quotes captures it: "How consumers come to learn about, desire, acquire, use, and dispose of goods, services, and activities available in the marketplace to satisfy their needs."
- Consumer psychology asks why, in terms of mental processes: attention, memory, motivation, emotion and judgment.
- Behavioral economics starts from economic models of choice and adds the ways real people depart from them, such as loss aversion (Kahneman & Tversky, 1979) or the pull of the status quo (Samuelson & Zeckhauser, 1988).

For the "what" side, see [what consumer behavior is and how people decide](/insights/what-is-consumer-behavior) and my [consumer behavior consulting](/consumer-behavior).

## How do I use consumer psychology in my own work?

[Optimolds](/work/optimolds) is an Italy-based composite engineering and mold-making firm whose buyers are cautious for a good reason: a bad mold ruins a production run. That's row 6, weighing risk, so the answer had to reduce the risk, not add claims. In the trilingual website and lead-generation funnel I designed and built, the one clear call to action is a free 48-hour feasibility check, with trust signals beside it (reviews, reply time, accepted CAD formats, an Italian base) and a short CAD-upload form to cut the effort.

[OopsCupid](/work/oopscupid), the relationship-assessment platform I designed and launched, raised a question about motivation and effort. People arrive curious, and a sign-up form before the first result costs effort just when curiosity peaks, so I removed sign-up as a design decision. Its 15 free research-informed tests are scored by fixed rules into a six-axis psychometric profile. Without an email list, daily challenges and streaks have to bring people back.

Pick one real purchase of your own product and answer the eight questions from the customer's side, in their words. Wherever you can only guess, you've found your first research question.
