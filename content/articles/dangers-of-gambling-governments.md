Gambling's danger is that it's designed to work on everyone's psychology, while its harm falls hardest on the people who can least afford it. And it's spreading faster than at any time I can remember: betting apps in every pocket, sports betting legal across most of the United States, prediction markets that take bets on elections, games that sell random rewards, and governments that don't just regulate gambling but run it, and even use it as a policy tool.

I'm an applied psychologist in Milan, and I built [Dopamine Box](/work/dopamine-box), a behavioral experiment on betting that runs on mock tokens with no real money, to study how people bet. This is what I think everyone should know about where gambling is going and why it works so well.

## How is gambling spreading?

In the United States, the turning point was a court case. On May 14, 2018, in Murphy v. NCAA, the Supreme Court struck down the federal law that had kept most states from legalizing sports betting. By the end of 2025, legal sports betting was offered in 39 states plus Washington, DC, according to the American Gaming Association.

The bigger change is where gambling lives. A casino used to be a place you traveled to; now it fits in a phone and never closes. Prediction markets have added a new front. In September 2024 a federal court in Washington, DC, ruled that the Commodity Futures Trading Commission could not bar Kalshi's contracts on which party would control Congress, and from January 2025 Kalshi and other platforms offered sports event contracts, which many states treat as unlicensed sports betting. By autumn 2026 federal appeals courts were split, and New Jersey's petition was pending at the US Supreme Court.

Games have their own version. Paid loot boxes, random rewards bought with real money, sit inside games played by children and adults alike. In 2018 Belgium's Gaming Commission concluded that the paid loot boxes in several major games were games of chance that needed a gambling licence. A survey of 7,422 gamers by Zendle and Cairns (2018) found that loot-box spending was linked to problem-gambling severity, more strongly than other spending in games, though a survey like this can't show which causes which.

## Why do governments run gambling?

Governments are some of gambling's biggest operators. New Hampshire sold the first tickets of the first modern US state lottery on March 12, 1964; today 45 states run one. The United Kingdom's National Lottery held its first draw on November 19, 1994.

Lotteries raise money without a formal tax, and the money is often tied to good causes such as education. The cost is unevenly shared. Charles Clotfelter and Philip Cook, in Selling Hope (1989), found the implicit lottery tax regressive in virtually all cases: lower-income households spend a larger share of their income on tickets, and later studies broadly agree.

Italy shows the tension clearly. Its 2018 Decreto Dignità banned almost all advertising, direct or indirect, for games and bets with cash prizes, on any medium, and banned gambling sponsorships from 2019. Among the exemptions are national lotteries with deferred draws. In 2026 the Constitutional Court upheld the ban, while allowing lower fines where the minimum would be clearly disproportionate. That's a summary for readers, not legal advice.

## How do governments use chance as a policy tool?

Governments also use lotteries to change behavior, and here the details matter, because not every use of chance costs citizens anything:

| Policy | How it works | What citizens risk | What we know |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Italy's receipt lottery (from 2021) | Cashless purchases earn entries to prize draws, part of the Italia Cashless program | Nothing: you take part by paying electronically | A nudge toward payments that leave a record |
| Taiwan's uniform-invoice lottery (since 1951) | Receipts double as lottery tickets, introduced to boost tax revenue | Nothing | It has run for decades |
| Ohio's Vax-a-Million (2021) | Vaccinated adults entered $1 million draws; teenagers could win college scholarships | Nothing | Mixed: one JAMA study found no evidence of higher adult vaccination; other analyses estimated modest gains |
| UK Premium Bonds (since 1956) | Savings that pay prizes in monthly draws instead of interest | No stake is lost, though inflation erodes its value | Savers can cash in at face value |
| State lotteries | Tickets fund the state | The price of every ticket | Regressive in virtually all cases (Clotfelter & Cook, 1989) |

The lesson I take from this table is that the thrill of chance and the harm of gambling can be separated. A receipt lottery or a prize-linked savings account uses chance without asking anyone to lose a stake. A lottery ticket bought every week does.

## Why is gambling so compelling?

Gambling works because it's built on some of the most reliable effects in psychology. Rewards that come on an unpredictable schedule produce some of the highest and most persistent response rates of any reinforcement schedule (Ferster & Skinner, 1957). Every spin might be the one.

Near-misses deepen the pull. Luke Clark and colleagues (2009) found that near-misses felt less pleasant than clear misses, yet increased the desire to keep playing when players felt in control, and they activated brain circuitry that also responds to wins. Michael Dixon and colleagues (2010) studied "losses disguised as wins" on multi-line slot machines: outcomes that pay back less than the bet but are celebrated with lights and sounds. Players' physical arousal to these outcomes resembled their response to real wins.

Natasha Dow Schüll, in Addiction by Design (2012), showed how machine gambling is engineered for continuous play, and described the "machine zone" that players seek: a state in which the goal is no longer to win but to keep playing. Add loss aversion, the tendency for losses to loom larger than gains (Kahneman & Tversky, 1979), and you get the urge to chase losses, one more bet to win back the last one.

## Who gets hurt?

In 2024 the Lancet Public Health Commission on gambling concluded that gambling poses a threat to public health and should be treated as a public health issue, with tighter regulation and limits on marketing. It estimated that about 448.7 million adults worldwide experience some form of risky gambling, and about 80 million experience gambling disorder or problematic gambling.

Gambling disorder is a recognized condition. In DSM-5 (2013) it is the only behavioral addiction in the chapter on substance-related and addictive disorders. And the harm concentrates: on heavy players, on lower-income households through lotteries, and on young people who can meet gambling's mechanics first inside games.

## What did building a betting experiment teach me?

Dopamine Box was built to observe how people bet compared with the Kelly criterion, the mathematically optimal stake, and which attitudes and biases pull them off course. It runs on a "spin to claim" loop with variable rewards, near-misses and scarcity tags, all with mock tokens. I explain its mechanics in [variable rewards and near-misses](/insights/variable-rewards-near-miss-ethics).

Building it taught me how cheap these mechanics are to add and how deliberately each one works on attention. That's why they stay in a research prototype with mock tokens, and why I hold my own products to the rules I describe in [engaging, not addictive](/insights/engaging-not-addictive-design).

## What would safer gambling look like?

If gambling is a public health issue, design is part of the cure. In my view, these would make the biggest difference:

1. Show real money, always: no chips, credits or gems that hide what a bet costs.
2. Don't celebrate losses. A loss disguised as a win should look like the loss it is.
3. Don't engineer near-misses. If an outcome is random, the display shouldn't make it look closer than it was.
4. Add friction where it protects: deposit limits set in advance, cooling-off periods and a pause before another deposit. I write about this kind of friction in [positive friction](/insights/positive-friction-ux-design).
5. Never sell randomness to children, inside games or anywhere else.

Gambling isn't going away, and many people gamble without harm. But a technology this effective at holding attention, now available everywhere, sold by companies and by governments alike, deserves the same seriousness we bring to anything else that can hurt people. I'd rather we design for the people it harms than pretend they're rare.
