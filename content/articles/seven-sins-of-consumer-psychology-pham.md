The seven sins of consumer psychology are seven research habits that, in Michel Tuan Pham's view, make the field less relevant than it should be: to businesses, policy makers and consumers, and even to other researchers. In a 2013 article in the Journal of Consumer Psychology, Pham named them: a narrow scope, narrow theoretical lenses, a narrow epistemology, a disregard for content, overgeneralization, research by convenience, and confusing "theories of studies" with studies of theories.

I read Pham from the other side of the gap he describes. I'm a behavioral scientist and consumer psychology consultant, not an academic, and in my experience the same habits show up in company research, including mine.

## What is "The Seven Sins of Consumer Psychology"?

It's an invited article by Michel Tuan Pham of Columbia Business School, published as an editorial in the Journal of Consumer Psychology (Pham, 2013; volume 23, issue 4, pages 411–423; doi:10.1016/j.jcps.2013.07.004). It grew out of his outgoing presidential address to the Society for Consumer Psychology in San Antonio, Texas, in March 2013. The title, he notes, was inspired by Daniel Schacter's 1999 article "The Seven Sins of Memory".

Pham's starting point is relevance, on two fronts. Externally, [consumer psychology](/insights/what-is-consumer-psychology) says too little that is useful to businesses, policy makers and consumers. Internally, much of it is barely used by other researchers: by his estimate, most Journal of Consumer Research articles from 2004 to 2008 are rarely cited.

## What are the seven sins of consumer psychology?

In Pham's order, with his wording from the abstract and the remedies from his concluding roadmap:

1. **Narrow scope:** "a narrow conception of the scope of consumer behavior research". Research concentrates on buying and its proxies (attitudes, choice, willingness to pay) and neglects how needs arise, how products are used, other ways of acquiring things (borrowing, sharing, renting, gifts, even theft) and disposal. Remedy: study those stages too.
2. **Narrow lenses:** "adoption of a narrow set of theoretical lenses". Cognitive psychology, social cognition and behavioral decision theory dominate, producing a "mechanistic view" of the consumer; a related fault is an "obsession with unique theoretical explanations". Remedy: widen the lens to emotion, motivation, personality, groups and families, and cultural and evolutionary psychology, and accept that several explanations can coexist.
3. **Narrow epistemology:** "adherence to a narrow epistemology of consumer research". The theory-driven, hypothetico-deductive path dominates. Remedy: also accept inductive, phenomenon-driven work, if the phenomenon is reliable and truly about consumers, and descriptive "empirical generalization" research. He also proposes "field-theory validation": rigorously testing the theories of industry consultants, as Martin (2012) did with Underhill's "butt-brush" effect.
4. **Disregard for content:** "an almost exclusive emphasis on psychological processes as opposed to psychological content". Researchers study mechanisms and ignore what consumers actually think, feel, want and value. His content-rich examples include the "unhealthy = tasty" research of Raghunathan and colleagues (2006) and social-norm appeals to reuse hotel towels (Goldstein et al., 2008). Remedy: put content at the center, suspend the search for universals and ground theory in specific consumption contexts.
5. **Overgeneralization:** "a strong tendency to overgeneralize from finite empirical results, both as authors and as reviewers". Published findings get treated as "gospel". His example is the jam study behind "too much choice" (Iyengar & Lepper, 2000): of the 63 studies in the meta-analysis by Scheibehenne, Greifeneder and Todd (2010), he notes, only 16 found the effect and 7 found the reverse. Remedy, which he calls "promoting knowledge calibration": more replications, larger samples, more nuance, transparent methods.
6. **Research by convenience:** "a predisposition to design studies based on methodological convenience rather than on substantive considerations". Studies lean too heavily on college students, MTurk workers, North American samples and imagined-scenario vignettes, and cheap online data can steer what gets studied. Remedy: field studies with real consumers and real behavior, broader populations, and more care with vignettes, especially for "hot" emotional states.
7. **Theories of studies:** "a pervasive confusion between 'theories of studies' and studies of theories". Papers that look like theory tests often conceptualize narrow effects that exist only in the authors' contrived lab setups. Remedy: less tolerance for them. In his words: "Studies should provide simplified models of the consumption world, not conceptual models of nothing."

## What else does Pham recommend?

Beyond the seven remedies, Pham asks for structural change: more applied, MBA-level courses and teaching in doctoral training; researchers who increase "their physical exposure to businesses, policy agencies, and actual consumers… through consulting, executive teaching, and field visits"; and incentives "rebalanced" toward real research impact rather than the "number of A's", meaning top-journal publications.

For practitioners, field-theory validation cuts both ways: what consultants believe is worth testing, and unproven until tested. My own Four Gates model of why customers hesitate is that kind of theory: by Pham's standard, a set of hypotheses, not a finding.

## How do the seven sins show up in company research?

Pham wrote about academic research, but in my view every sin has a company version. The table is my translation, not his. I call it the seven-sins audit.

| Pham's sin | How it shows up in company research | What I do about it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Narrow scope | Research stops at the purchase, ignoring the need before it and the use and cancellation after. | Map desire, acquisition, use and disposal, and talk to people after the sale. |
| Narrow lenses | One favorite bias explains every problem. | Check cognitive, emotional, motivational, social and cultural explanations; keep two alive until the data separates them. |
| Narrow epistemology | Only A/B tests count; interviews are "anecdotes". | Explore, then describe, then test, and treat pet theories, mine included, as hypotheses. |
| Disregard for content | The team can name the mechanism but not what customers want, fear or value. | Write the doubt or desire in the customer's words before picking a mechanism. |
| Overgeneralization | A famous study becomes a rule: "fewer options always convert better". | Treat a published effect as a hypothesis here, and size the test for a small effect. |
| Convenience | Tests with colleagues, friends or a cheap panel; "would you buy this?" surveys. | Recruit from the real market and watch behavior, especially for emotional decisions. |
| Theories of studies | An "insight" that exists only inside one test setup. | Check that the test models the real decision: the real people, stakes and setting. |

In my view, companies import overgeneralization most directly: a famous effect becomes a design rule before anyone checks it with their own customers. Nudges show the pattern: [effects tested at scale by nudge units are much smaller than in published studies](/insights/do-nudges-work-evidence-for-product-teams) (DellaVigna & Linos, 2022).

## Where does Pham's critique apply to my own experiment?

It's easy to recognize other people's sins, so here is my own work against the list. My M.Sc. thesis was an automated interview simulation in which each of 96 participants answered in three formats: self-view, an AI avatar and text only. A repeated-measures ANOVA with Holm-corrected post hoc tests showed they were significantly more expressive with the AI avatar than when they saw themselves, p = .009. The details are in [my write-up of the self-view experiment](/insights/ai-interview-self-view-experiment) and the [interview simulation case study](/work/interview-simulation-study).

One thing holds up. The manipulation mirrors a real design choice, since many video tools show people their own image by default, so it isn't a model of nothing.

Three things are exposed:

- **Convenience.** It was a simulation, so nobody's job depended on it. Pham asks for more care with vignettes in "hot" emotional states; a simulation is closer to reality, but a real interview is hotter still.
- **Overgeneralization.** It's one study, with one sample, in one simulated setting. "Avatars make candidates more expressive" would be exactly the overreach he warns about.
- **Narrow lenses.** I explain the result with self-awareness: seeing yourself makes you self-conscious, while an avatar gives you someone to talk to. That's an interpretation, not a result, and other explanations could coexist.

Pham's remedy applies: replication with real candidates, other avatars and other populations.

## How do you do consumer research with fewer sins?

In client work, my safeguard is to combine methods whose weaknesses differ. For [Musicity](/work/musicity), a music platform with three segments, I ran semi-structured interviews with artists, listeners and venues and coded them thematically into a ranked jobs-to-be-done view of demand, weighting what people did over what they said when the two conflicted. That covers more than the purchase (sin 1), builds its ranking inductively rather than testing a prior hypothesis (sin 3), centers what people are trying to get done (sin 4) and talks to the real market (sin 6). The method is in [jobs-to-be-done interviews for market fit](/insights/jobs-to-be-done-market-fit-interviews).

Its weak spot mirrors my thesis: interviews are rich in content and thin on control, so I treat any ranking they produce as a strong hypothesis, not proof. Exploring with interviews and then testing with behavior is how I approach [behavioral science consulting](/behavioral-science), and the seven-sins audit is how I check research plans in [consumer psychology projects](/consumer-psychology).

Before your team's next study, put the seven sins next to the plan as questions: which stage of consumption does it ignore, which explanation did we assume, who is in the sample and why, and would the effect survive outside this setup, with real stakes? If nobody can answer the last one, you may be about to produce a theory of a study rather than a study of your customers.
