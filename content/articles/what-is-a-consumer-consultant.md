A consumer consultant can be any of four quite different jobs, depending on who is using the phrase. In business it usually means a consultant who helps companies understand their customers, or one who specializes in consumer-goods and retail companies; in Australian mental health services it means a person with lived experience, employed to bring the perspective of the people who use a service into how it's run; and occasionally it appears as a sales title.

So the first step is working out which one you need. I'm the first kind, a behavioral scientist and consumer psychology consultant in Milan, and most of this article is about that meaning. The other three deserve a fair explanation, though, and the table below should tell you quickly which one fits your situation.

## What are the four meanings of "consumer consultant"?

### 1. A consultant who advises businesses on their consumers

This is the business meaning: someone hired to find out why customers buy, hesitate or leave, and what to change as a result. The titles vary: consumer insights consultant, consumer psychology consultant, consumer behavior consultant, market research consultant. The work combines research, such as interviews, surveys, analytics and experiments, with advice on products, offers, messages and launches.

### 2. A consultant who specializes in consumer-goods and retail companies

In management consulting, "consumer" can name an industry practice. The client is a company that sells to consumers, such as a food, beauty or retail brand, and the subject can be anything from strategy to operations. Bain & Company, for example, markets "Consumer Products Consulting Services". These consultants may or may not study consumers directly; their specialty is the sector.

### 3. A lived-experience role in mental health services

In Australian public mental health services, "consumer" means a person who uses the service, and a consumer consultant belongs to the lived-experience workforce. The Royal Melbourne Hospital describes the role like this: "Consumer or carer consultants focus on service improvement… provide information and feedback from consumers or carers about their views and experiences of the service and use this information to make recommendations for service improvement."

The Victorian Department of Health adds that lived and living experience workers "may provide support directly… or indirectly through leadership, consultation, system advocacy, training or research."

New Zealand uses different words. Te Pou speaks of the "consumer, peer support and lived experience (CPSLE) workforce", and typical titles there appear to be consumer advisor or lived experience advisor rather than consumer consultant. In both countries, "consumer" here has nothing to do with shopping.

### 4. A customer-facing sales title

If you've seen the phrase in a job ad for a store, a bank or a phone company, it's likely a sales or customer-service role. That use is rare; the usual titles for those jobs are "customer consultant" and "sales consultant".

A near neighbor causes confusion too. If you have a problem with something you bought, the person who helps is usually called a consumer adviser, as on the Citizens Advice consumer service in the UK, not a consultant.

## What does a consumer insights consultant do?

In the business meaning, the job is to turn evidence about customers into a decision. I separate three flavors, although the titles overlap in practice and the same person can do all three:

- Consumer insights: what's true about your customers that you didn't know, and what it means for a decision. I explain how an insight differs from a finding in [what market insights are](/insights/what-are-market-insights).
- Consumer behavior: what customers actually do, step by step, and where they stop.
- Consumer psychology: why they do it, in terms of the doubts, motives and biases behind the choice.

The methods are shared. Interviews are often built around the progress a customer is trying to make, the "job to be done" (Christensen, Hall, Dillon & Duncan, 2016). What people say gets coded systematically, for example with thematic analysis (Braun & Clarke, 2006). Surveys, behavioral data and small experiments fill in the rest.

The output should be a decision someone can act on: which segment first, which offer, which change to the funnel, and how you'll know it worked. You can see how I run this work on my [market research](/market-research) and [consumer psychology consulting](/consumer-psychology) pages.

## What does a first engagement look like?

It should be small enough to finish and specific enough to act on. Before I start with a new client, I ask for four things:

1. The decision, in one sentence: "Which segment do we launch to?" or "Why do people abandon the quote form?"
2. What you already have: analytics, sales notes, support emails, past research. In my experience, most teams have more evidence than they think.
3. Access to customers: a handful of real buyers, or people who almost bought, to interview.
4. A measure: the number that should move if the recommendation is right.

What comes back is a short readout: what we found, how confident we are and why, what to change, and what to measure. For a website or a funnel, it takes the form of the step-by-step audit I describe in [what a consumer psychology consultant does](/insights/what-does-a-consumer-psychology-consultant-do).

## How do you judge a consumer consultant?

In the business meaning, look for things you can check rather than adjectives:

- A stated method and a measure: they can tell you how they'd answer your question and which number should move.
- A clear line between what people say and what they actually do.
- Honesty about training and titles. In my case: I'm not a psychologist. In Italy the title is protected, so I work as a behavioral scientist and consumer psychology consultant, trained in experimental psychology at the University of Milano-Bicocca.
- Relevant work you can examine, with what was done and why.
- Willingness to tell you when you don't need them.

I look at what research says about how people choose consultants in [what makes a good consultant](/insights/what-makes-a-good-consultant-research).

## What does this look like in my work?

Two projects show the business meaning in practice.

For [Optimolds](/work/optimolds), an Italy-based composite engineering and mold-making firm, the consumer question was about risk: its buyers are cautious because a bad mold ruins a production run. So the deliverable wasn't a report but a different first step. I designed and built the trilingual website and lead-generation funnel so that contacting the firm costs a buyer nothing: a free 48-hour feasibility check, a short CAD-upload form, and reviews, reply time and accepted CAD formats shown next to that one call to action.

For [Musicity](/work/musicity), a music platform with three segments (artists, listeners and venues), the deliverable was a decision: which segment to win first. I assessed market fit through interviews across all three, coded them into a ranked jobs-to-be-done view of demand, and recommended the first segment, the positioning and the launch order. The method is in [jobs-to-be-done interviews for market fit](/insights/jobs-to-be-done-market-fit-interviews); which segment came first stays with the client.

Before you contact anyone, write one sentence: the decision you face and what you'd need to know to make it. If the sentence is about your customers' choices, you want the first kind of consumer consultant; if it's about your industry, the second; if it's about a mental health service, the third. If it's the first kind, I'm glad to talk it through.
