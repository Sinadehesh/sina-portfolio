Blogging continued a celebration that began with Gutenberg's printing press: the celebration of collective knowledge, experience and creativity. The press made knowledge cheap to copy and send far, so ideas could reach strangers. The blog removed the last gatekeeper, so anyone could add their own. Between the two, knowledge went from something a few people kept to something everyone could read, and then something everyone could write.

I write this on a blog, and I'm designing [Pukytoon](/work/pukytoon), a feed of games anyone can make, because I think games are the next chapter of the same story. Here's how I see the line from Mainz to the comment box, and what it teaches anyone building tools for people to make things.

## What did Gutenberg's printing press change?

Johannes Gutenberg developed printing with movable metal type in the mid-fifteenth century, experimenting first in Strasbourg and perfecting the press in Mainz. His Bible was finished by around 1455. The technique spread fast: by 1500, printing workshops had appeared in around 250 places across Europe.

The historian Elizabeth Eisenstein (1979) argued that the deepest change wasn't speed but what print did to knowledge itself. Copies became identical, so readers in different cities could discuss the same page. They became plentiful, so ideas reached far beyond the few who could afford a manuscript. And they became durable, because a text that existed in hundreds of copies was hard to lose. In her account, those changes fed the Reformation, the Renaissance and the Scientific Revolution.

## How did print turn knowledge into a conversation?

The press didn't only spread knowledge. It let people answer each other in public, across distance. In 1517 Martin Luther's Ninety-five Theses were reprinted, without his permission, in Leipzig, Nuremberg and Basel within the year, and pamphlets made up most of the surge of printing that followed. A pamphlet could be answered with a pamphlet, and it was.

Science learned the same habit. In 1665 two journals began within months of each other: the Journal des sçavans in January and the Philosophical Transactions of the Royal Society in March, which the Royal Society calls the world's first and longest-running scientific journal. People could now report what they had observed and let strangers check it. A century later, Diderot and d'Alembert's Encyclopédie gathered the work of many contributors into one great reference, its text volumes appearing from 1751 and its plates until 1772.

That's what I mean by a celebration of collective knowledge. Print made it possible for what one person knew, saw or made to become part of what everyone could use, and to be argued with, corrected and built on.

## Why was every new medium feared?

Every time the ability to share widened, someone worried it would do harm. In Plato's Phaedrus, Socrates tells a myth in which an Egyptian king warns that writing will produce forgetfulness in the minds of those who learn to use it. In 1545 the Swiss scholar Conrad Gessner complained of "that confusing and harmful abundance of books."

The worries weren't foolish. More voices really do mean more noise. But each time, people built new ways to find what mattered: catalogs, indexes and reviews after print, and links, comments and search after the web. Gessner's own answer was the very book in which he complained: a vast catalog of the books he could find. The flood was real; so was the human talent for building filters.

## What did blogging add?

The web gave print's descendants a new home. Tim Berners-Lee proposed the system that became the World Wide Web at CERN in March 1989. Within a decade, people were keeping public logs of what they found and thought. Jorn Barger coined "weblog" in December 1997; in May 1999 Peter Merholz joked that he would pronounce it "we blog," and the short form stuck. Blogger launched that August, and WordPress was first released in May 2003. Wikipedia, launched on January 15, 2001, turned the encyclopedia itself into something anyone could write.

Blogging removed the last gatekeeper between a person and the public. A printer had to choose to print you; a blog needed nobody's permission. Clay Shirky (2008) described the shift as a move from "filter, then publish" to "publish, then filter": instead of editors deciding in advance what was worth printing, everything could be published, and readers, links and search decided what rose.

## How did blogging celebrate experience?

Print celebrated what experts knew. Blogging added what everyone had lived. People wrote about their illnesses, their children, their kitchens, their repairs and their travels, and the experience of strangers became something you could search. The recipe that worked at high altitude, the honest account of a hospital stay, the step the manual forgot: knowledge that would never have reached a printing press finally had a home.

Why would people do that for free? Part of the answer is in the brain. Diana Tamir and Jason Mitchell (2012) found that disclosing information about ourselves is intrinsically rewarding: it engages the brain's reward system, and in their experiments people were even willing to give up money to talk about themselves. Add reciprocity, the urge to give back what we've received (Cialdini, 1984), and the sense that our contributions matter, which Henry Jenkins and colleagues (2006) put at the center of participatory culture, and the generosity of the early web makes sense.

## Three times knowledge opened up

Seen side by side, the press, the blog and today's game-making tools follow the same pattern:

| | Gutenberg's press | Blogging | Making games |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| What became cheap | Copying a text | Publishing a text | Making a playable game |
| Who could take part | Anyone who could read | Anyone who could write | Anyone with an idea |
| What it celebrated | Shared knowledge | Shared experience | Shared play |
| The worry | Too many books | Too many voices | Too many games |
| The filters that followed | Catalogs, indexes, reviews | Links, comments, search | Feeds, credit, followers |

## What comes after the blog?

I think the next chapter is play. AI and no-code tools are doing for games what blogging software did for writing: removing the specialist between an idea and the public. A game can carry what a blog post carries, a piece of knowledge, an experience, a joke, a way of seeing, but it lets the reader do something with it. The press taught us that knowledge grows when it's shared. The blog taught us that everyone has something worth sharing. I think games will teach us that everyone has something worth playing.
