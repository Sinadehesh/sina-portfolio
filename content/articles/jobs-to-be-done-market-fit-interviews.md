Jobs-to-be-done interviews are the most reliable way I know to test market fit across several customer segments, because they ask about what people already did, not what they say they'd like. Code those conversations into jobs, rank the jobs by the strength of behavioral evidence, and the choice of a beachhead segment becomes an argument you can show rather than a hunch.

I used this approach with Musicity, a music platform with three segments (artists, listeners and venues) whose needs pulled in different directions. I won't reveal which segment came first, because that is the client's strategy. But the method travels to any product with more than one kind of customer.

## What is a jobs-to-be-done interview?

Christensen, Hall, Dillon and Duncan (2016) describe buying as hiring: people "hire" a product to make progress in a particular circumstance, and "fire" whatever they used before. A job has functional, social and emotional sides. The manager who needs a report also needs to look prepared in the meeting.

A JTBD interview reconstructs one real episode of that progress. The core questions:

- "Tell me about the last time you tried to do this. Where were you, and what set it off?"
- "What did you use? What else did you consider?"
- "What was frustrating about it, and what did you do about that?"
- "Have you spent anything to make it easier: money, time, favors?"
- "What would have to be true for you to switch?"

The rule underneath is to ask about the past, not the future. "Would you use an app that…?" invites a polite yes. "What did you do last Saturday?" doesn't.

## How do you separate stated wants from real demand?

The hardest part of market-fit interviews is that people are generous with opinions. Everyone likes the idea. So I rank every piece of evidence on what I call the say/do ladder:

| Rung | Evidence | Example | Weight |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Opinion | "That sounds great." | Ignore |
| 2 | Stated intent | "I'd definitely use that." | Very low |
| 3 | Specific past struggle | "Last month I lost a whole evening to this." | Medium |
| 4 | Workaround | "I built a spreadsheet to handle it." | High |
| 5 | Money or switching | "I pay for two tools and still do part of it by hand." | Highest |

Demand lives on rungs three to five. A segment full of rung-one enthusiasm and no workarounds is telling you the problem isn't painful enough to act on. When stated wants and behavior conflict, weight the behavior. That was the rule I followed for Musicity.

## How do you rank jobs across segments that conflict?

On a multi-sided platform there's no average user. Blend three segments into one persona and you get someone who wants everything and exists nowhere. So the analysis runs in two passes.

First, within each segment. Code every interview into jobs, attach the evidence for each job with its rung on the ladder, and rank the segment's jobs by how high their evidence climbs, then by how many people share them. This is thematic analysis (Braun & Clarke, 2006) with a jobs-to-be-done codebook.

Second, across segments. Compare each segment's strongest jobs on the same criteria, and note how the jobs depend on each other. The result is a ranked jobs-to-be-done view: one page that shows where demand is strongest.

A hypothetical example shows the shape. Imagine a tutoring marketplace with students, parents and tutors:

| Segment | Strongest job (invented) | Highest rung | Depends on |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Students | "Get unstuck on tonight's homework" | 3: specific struggles | Tutors being available |
| Parents | "Know my child is getting real help" | 5: already paying for tutoring | Students actually using it |
| Tutors | "Fill empty hours without chasing clients" | 4: juggling several platforms | Demand from parents |

In that invented case, parents show the strongest behavioral demand, but their job depends on students using the product. The launch has to serve one segment first without ignoring the dependency.

## How do you choose a beachhead segment?

Once the jobs are ranked, the go-to-market question becomes concrete. I score candidate beachheads on four criteria, a simple beachhead scorecard:

1. **Demand:** how high does the evidence for the segment's top job climb on the say/do ladder?
2. **Reach:** can you find and talk to these people cheaply, through channels you already have?
3. **Gap:** how badly do current solutions serve the job?
4. **Pull:** does winning this segment make the next one easier? On a platform, does it bring the other side along?

Then pick one. On a platform the temptation is to launch to every segment at once, because every side seems necessary. In my view that spreads a small team across conflicting needs and slows learning. Leading with the segment that shows the strongest demand should lower acquisition cost and speed up learning, which is the hypothesis I worked from with Musicity.

## How I ran market-fit interviews for Musicity

Musicity had three segments with conflicting needs and no evidence about which one to lead with. My work in August 2026 ran in four steps:

1. **One protocol across three segments.** A semi-structured interview protocol used with artists, listeners and venues, so jobs could be compared across groups.
2. **Thematic coding into jobs.** Each interview coded into the jobs people were trying to get done and the evidence behind them.
3. **A ranked view of demand.** The coded jobs combined into a ranked jobs-to-be-done view by segment, with behavioral evidence weighted over stated preference where the two conflicted.
4. **A go-to-market sequence.** Recommendations on which segment to win first, how to position for it and the launch order.

The recommendation was to focus on one beachhead segment instead of launching to all three at once. Which one, and why, belongs to Musicity. The method is summarized on the [Musicity case study](/work/musicity) page.

## What mistakes make market-fit interviews misleading?

- **Asking about features.** You get a wish list, not a job.
- **Recruiting friends and fans.** They start on rung one by default.
- **Counting mentions as demand.** Ten people mentioning a problem in passing is weaker evidence than three who built a workaround.
- **Averaging across segments.** The conflicts between segments are often the most important finding, so don't smooth them away.
- **Pitching during the interview.** The moment you describe your solution, the conversation turns into politeness.
