Consumer behavior is what people do as they notice a need, look for options, choose, buy, use, and then repeat, switch or drop a product or service, together with the influences that shape each of those steps. It answers the question every business eventually asks: what do customers actually do, and what drives it?

I work on that question as a behavioral scientist and consumer psychology consultant in Milan. Below: the decision process and where it breaks, the factors behind it, the ideas that explain most purchases, how to study behavior rather than claims, and the decision timeline I use.

## What does consumer behavior cover?

A definition quoted by Michel Tuan Pham (2013) puts the scope well: how consumers "come to learn about, desire, acquire, use, and dispose of goods, services, and activities available in the marketplace to satisfy their needs." Buying is only one of five verbs. One of Pham's criticisms of his own field was that research concentrates on buying and neglects desire, use and disposal. In my experience companies make the same mistake: they study the checkout and ignore the weeks before the search and the month after delivery.

Consumer behavior (consumer behaviour, in British spelling) is also broader than consumer psychology. Consumer psychology asks why, through perception, memory, motivation, emotion and judgment; consumer behavior adds the social, cultural and economic forces around a purchase, and the patterns you can see in purchase data. I cover the "why" side in [what consumer psychology is](/insights/what-is-consumer-psychology).

## What is the consumer decision-making process?

Marketing textbooks, including Kotler and Armstrong's Principles of Marketing, describe five stages:

1. **Need recognition:** something makes the current situation feel wrong or improvable.
2. **Information search:** asking people, searching, reading reviews.
3. **Evaluation of alternatives:** building a shortlist and comparing it on a few criteria.
4. **Purchase decision:** choosing, and then actually paying.
5. **Post-purchase behavior:** satisfaction or regret, repeating, recommending, returning.

The sequence is the backbone of the "grand models" of consumer behavior from the late 1960s, above all the Engel, Kollat and Blackwell model (1968). Later editions of the Engel model add consumption and divestment as stages, which I find more useful for anything sold as a subscription. The sequence is often traced to John Dewey's book How We Think (1910), which describes five steps of reflective thought, from "a felt difficulty" to the observation that leads to accepting or rejecting a solution. Dewey was describing problem-solving in general, not shopping.

Real decisions are messier. Repeat purchases often run on habit, with no search or evaluation at all, and impulse purchases jump from need to purchase. Expensive or risky purchases loop: a review sends someone back to search, a quote sends them back to evaluation, and in B2B the person who evaluates isn't always the person who signs. I treat the five stages as a checklist of where a decision can stall, not as a route every customer follows.

## What factors influence consumer behavior?

Kotler and Armstrong sort the influences into four groups: cultural, social, personal and psychological. In my own words:

- **Cultural:** the values, norms and tastes people absorb from where and how they grew up.
- **Social:** family, friends, colleagues and the groups people compare themselves with.
- **Personal:** age and stage of life, work, income, lifestyle and self-image.
- **Psychological:** motivation, perception, learning, beliefs and attitudes.

When a team explains a purchase with only one group, usually the psychological one, I ask what the other three would say. A B2B buyer's caution is partly psychology and partly the job: they answer to someone if the supplier fails.

## Which ideas explain most real purchases?

No single theory explains buying, but in my work six ideas do most of the explaining. The first comes from Herbert Simon, who argued in 1955 that people choose with the information and computing capacity they actually have, not the "global rationality of economic man"; he gave "satisfice" its modern sense in 1956, and the phrase "bounded rationality" comes from his 1957 book Models of Man.

| Idea | Where it comes from | What it means for a business |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Good enough beats best | Bounded rationality and satisficing (Simon) | Customers often stop at the first option that clears their bar, so be easy to find and clearly good enough |
| Losses loom larger | Prospect theory (Kahneman & Tversky, 1979); losses weigh roughly twice as much as equivalent gains in typical estimates (Tversky & Kahneman, 1992) | The fear of a bad purchase can outweigh the appeal of a good one, so reduce the risk before adding benefits |
| Other people are evidence | Social proof (Cialdini, 1984) | When unsure, people follow people like them, so show proof where the doubt appears |
| Doing nothing wins | Status quo bias (Samuelson & Zeckhauser, 1988); defaults (Johnson & Goldstein, 2003; Madrian & Shea, 2001) | Defaults and habits carry much repeat behavior, so renewals and switching steps are where loyalty is decided |
| Doubt after buying | Cognitive dissonance (Festinger, 1957), which marketers apply as post-purchase dissonance | The moments after payment need reassurance: what happens next and how to get help |
| Big brands win twice | Double jeopardy (Ehrenberg, Goodhardt & Barwise, 1990); mental and physical availability (Sharp, 2010) | Small brands have fewer buyers, who also buy less often; Sharp argues growth comes mainly from being noticed and easy to buy |

## Why do customers say one thing and do another?

Because intentions predict behavior less well than we'd like. Sheeran (2002) pooled 10 meta-analyses covering 422 studies and 82,107 participants: intentions correlated with later behavior at r = .53, so they accounted for about 28% of the variance in what people did. That counts as a large effect, yet most of the behavior stays unexplained, mainly because of people who intend to act and don't.

Practitioners call this the say–do gap; researchers call it the intention–behavior gap. The studies are mostly about health and social behavior, not purchases, but I see the same gap in buying, which is why I give behavior more weight than stated preference when the two conflict. Companies study consumer behavior with five kinds of evidence, and turning them into decisions is what [market insights](/insights/what-are-market-insights) are for:

- **Behavioral data:** analytics, purchase and usage records, which show what people do and where they stop.
- **Interviews** about specific past decisions ("tell me about the last time you…"), not hypothetical ones.
- **Surveys**, for attitudes and how common something is, not for predicting purchases.
- **Observation and usability tests**, watching people use the thing.
- **Experiments**, the clearest way to show that a change caused a difference.

## What is the decision timeline?

The decision timeline is how I turn all of this into a plan. It follows the customer from trigger to repeat and asks, at each stage, what to measure and what to change first:

| Stage | What the customer is doing | What to measure | What to change first |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Trigger | Something makes the status quo feel wrong | What was happening when they started looking | Show up where the trigger happens, in the customer's words |
| Search | Asking, searching, reading reviews | Where they looked and which alternatives they found | Be easy to find, and easy to understand at a glance |
| Compare | Shortlisting on a few must-haves | The criteria they used, what they cut and why | Make the comparison easy on those criteria, with fewer, clearer options |
| Commit | Facing the price, the form and the risk | Step-to-step completion and the doubt in their own words | Answer each doubt where it appears |
| Use and stay | Judging the purchase, repeating or leaving | First use, repeat purchase, retention, returns | Reassure after payment and make the first use succeed |

The commit stage hides the most detail, because one unanswered doubt can undo a choice that was already made. Zoom in and you find the four doubts of my Four Gates model:

## How does consumer behavior show up in my work?

Three projects, three stages of the timeline:

- **Commit, in B2B.** [Optimolds](/work/optimolds) is an Italy-based composite engineering and mold-making firm whose buyers are risk-averse, because a bad mold ruins a production run. When I designed and built its trilingual website and lead-generation funnel, the behavior to change was contacting an unfamiliar supplier. The answers were a free 48-hour feasibility check as risk reversal, a short CAD-upload form to cut effort, and trust signals next to one clear call to action.
- **Need, across segments.** For [Musicity](/work/musicity), a music platform, I ran interviews with artists, listeners and venues and coded them into a ranked jobs-to-be-done view of demand. That view shaped my recommendation on which segment to win first, how to position for it and in which order to launch.
- **From first use to staying.** OopsCupid, my relationship-assessment platform, offers 15 free research-informed tests with no sign-up. Removing the form before the first result was a deliberate trade: I gave up email capture so that signing up wouldn't stand between people and their result. Daily challenges and streaks are there to bring them back.

A first step you can take this week: call five recent customers and walk each one back along the timeline, asking what was happening when they started looking, what else they considered, what nearly stopped them and what they did in the first week. The stage where their answers get vague, or where they pause, is where to measure next. It's also where my [consumer behavior consulting](/consumer-behavior) usually starts.
