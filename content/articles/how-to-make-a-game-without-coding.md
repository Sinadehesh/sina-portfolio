To make a game without coding, start small and start with a person. Pick one simple action a player repeats, decide who the game is for, build it with a no-code game maker or an AI chat tool, write rules you can say in one sentence, watch a few people play, and then share it. You can have a first version in an afternoon.

I design and test games and apps with real people, and I'm designing [Pukytoon](/work/pukytoon), a feed of games anyone can make, which is still in development. This is the path I'd give a friend who wants to make their first game.

## Step 1: Start with a game that already exists

Your first game shouldn't invent a new kind of game. Take one that players already understand and make it yours. Four simple mechanics carry an enormous number of games:

- Drop and merge: two of the same thing combine into something bigger.
- Endless runner: keep moving and dodge what's coming.
- Match-3: line up three of a kind.
- Whack-a-mole: tap things before they disappear.

Borrowing a mechanic isn't cheating; it's how most makers learn. Mitchel Resnick and colleagues (2009) designed Scratch to be tinkerable, and its online community lets people remix each other's projects. Changing something that already works is a gentle way into how it works. Your originality goes into the theme, the look, the rules and the joke.

## Step 2: Decide who it's for

Make your first game for one person or one small group: a friend's birthday, your class, your team at work, your family. A specific audience makes every decision easier. Which pictures? The ones they'll recognize. How hard? Hard enough to make them laugh, not quit.

It also makes the game matter more. Norton, Mochon and Ariely (2012) found that people value what they've made themselves, an effect named after IKEA. A game made for someone carries that value twice: you made it, and they know it was made for them.

## Step 3: Choose how you'll build it

There are three good routes for beginners:

| Route | How it works | Best for | Watch out for |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| A no-code game maker | You choose a game type, a look and the rules, and tune them while it plays | A polished first game in minutes | You're limited to the types the maker offers |
| An AI chat tool | You describe the game, then ask for changes until it plays the way you want | Unusual ideas no template covers | Results vary, and you'll need patience with changes |
| A block-based tool | You snap together blocks of logic, as in Scratch | Learning how games work inside | It takes longer to reach a finished game |

If you go the AI route, a short, structured description works better than a long one. This is the template I use:

```
Make a small game I can play on a phone, in portrait, with one thumb.
The player: [what they do, in one sentence].
Win: [how you win]. Lose: [how you lose].
Look: [colors, characters, mood].
Make the goal obvious within five seconds, give every tap a sound or a flash,
and keep a round under two minutes.
```

Then play it and ask for one change at a time. Small, specific requests ("make the obstacles slower for the first ten seconds") beat long lists.

## Step 4: Write the rules in one sentence

If you can't say how your game is won and lost in one sentence, it's too complicated for a first game. "Merge fruit until you make a watermelon, before the jar fills up." "Score 2,000 before the timer runs out." "Survive 90 seconds."

Then tune the difficulty. Mihaly Csikszentmihalyi (1990) described flow, the state of full absorption, as most likely when the challenge matches the person's skill. For a first game that means easy for the first few seconds, so players learn, then a little harder, so they stay. In Pukytoon's Creator Studio you can take a preset for winning and losing or write your own rule, like score ≥ 1000 or time ≥ 90, checked as you type.

## Step 5: Make every tap feel good

Players forgive simple pictures; they don't forgive silence. Every action should answer the player: a sound, a flash, a shake, a number that jumps. That feedback is what makes a game feel alive in the first seconds, before the player cares about winning.

Spend the same care on the ending. People judge an experience heavily by its peak and its end (Kahneman, Fredrickson, Schreiber & Redelmeier, 1993). A satisfying finish, with a big final merge, a final score that counts up or a funny line, is worth more than a second level.

## Step 6: Watch a few people play

This is the step most first-time makers skip, and the one I'd never skip. Give your game to a few people, say nothing, and watch. Jakob Nielsen (2000) argued that a handful of test users finds most usability problems, and in my experience of running interviews and usability tests, it holds for games too.

Watch for three things: where they hesitate, where they tap something that isn't a button, and where they stop. Then ask one question: "What were you trying to do there?" Change one thing and watch someone new.

## Step 7: Share it, and let people answer

Send it to the person you made it for first. Then share it where people look for games, not only in a private link, because a link only reaches people you already know. I wrote about why in [where AI-made games should live](/insights/ai-made-games-claude-artifacts).

The best response a game can get isn't a like. It's someone making their own version. If that happens, you're not just a person who made a game; you're a game creator with an audience, and the reasons people keep making are a story of their own, which I cover in [why people become game creators](/insights/why-people-make-games-creator-psychology).

## The first-game checklist

Before you share, check these seven things:

1. One mechanic, borrowed and made yours.
2. A named person or group it's for.
3. Rules you can say in one sentence.
4. A goal a stranger understands within five seconds.
5. Feedback on every tap.
6. An ending worth reaching.
7. At least three people have played it while you watched.
