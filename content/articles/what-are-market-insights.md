Market insights are explanations of why customers and markets behave the way they do, specific enough to change a business decision. Market research gathers and analyzes the evidence; the insight is what that evidence means for what you do next.

It's common to have plenty of research and little insight: dashboards, survey reports and interview notes that say what happened but not why, or what to change. Turning one into the other is a skill, and this is how I do it.

## What are market insights?

The definition I find most useful is Paul Laughlin's: customer insight is "a non-obvious understanding about your customers, which, if acted upon, has the potential to change their behavior for mutual benefit" (Laughlin, 2014). Its key phrases rule things out: what everyone already knew, curiosities nobody will act on, and tricks that only pay off for the company.

An ISBA guide endorsed by the MRS (2011) is blunter: "data on its own is virtually useless".

You'll also see "consumer insights": in my usage, insights about people, the motives and tensions behind what they do, which is the territory of [consumer behavior](/insights/what-is-consumer-behavior). Market insights also cover competitors, channels, pricing and where a category is heading. The standard is the same.

## How does data become an insight?

I picture a ladder with four rungs, and many reports stop on the second. Take a hypothetical invoicing app for small businesses whose free trials rarely turn into paying accounts:

| Rung | What it is | Hypothetical example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Data | What happened | Most trial accounts never connect a bank account, and those that don't rarely convert. |
| Finding | A pattern, with evidence | Owners say they'll connect "once my accountant has had a look"; support tickets often forward an accountant's question. |
| Insight | Why, and what it means | Owners treat the bank connection as their accountant's decision, so the trial stalls, waiting for someone who never sees the product. |
| Decision | What you'll do, and how you'll check | Let owners invite their accountant into the trial; test accountants as a channel before buying more ads aimed at owners. |

The data was about a button; the insight is about who decides. That's a market question, not a design one: it changes the channel, the message and perhaps the price plan.

## What's the difference between market research and market insights?

Market research is the process. The ICC/ESOMAR International Code (2025 edition) defines it: "Research, which includes all forms of market, opinion and social research, including data analytics applied for research purposes, means the systematic gathering, analysis and interpretation of information about individuals and organisations." It adds that research draws on the social, behavioral and data sciences "to generate insights and support decision-making", and the MRS Code of Conduct (2023) uses the same words.

By the profession's own codes, then, insight is what research produces, not another name for it. In practice they're often different jobs, with different standards:

| | Market research | Market insights |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Core question | What is true, and for how many? | Why, and what should we do? |
| Delivers | Survey results, sizing, segmentation, tracking | Insight statements, implications, a recommendation |
| Often done by | Research agencies and institutes, in-house research teams | Insights managers, strategists, product and marketing teams, consultants |
| Judged by | Rigor: sampling, measurement, accuracy | Consequence: did a decision change, and did it work? |

That's my framing, not an industry standard. Good practitioners do both, which is why job titles so often say "market research and insights." When do you need which?

- **Market research** when the decision depends on a number (how many, how much, what share) or has to convince a board.
- **Insight work** when you have data but no explanation, your numbers contradict each other, or a decision is stuck between opinions.
- **Both**, in my view, for most big decisions: qualitative work to find the why, quantitative research to measure how common it is, then a test.

## Where do market insights come from?

Rarely from one source: a non-obvious understanding usually needs evidence from several (Laughlin, 2014). Combine sources whose blind spots cancel out:

| Source | Best for | Blind spot |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Interviews | Motives, context, the words people use | Small samples; memory edits what happened |
| Surveys | How common a view or reported habit is | Answers only the questions you asked |
| Behavioral and sales data | What people actually do, at scale | Silent on why |
| Support tickets and reviews | Problems in customers' own words | Skews toward the unhappy and the vocal |
| Social listening | How people talk about a category or a rival | Skews toward people who post |
| Competitor benchmarking | How alternatives solve the same job | Shows offers, not needs |

The pairing I insist on is one source about what people say and one about what they do. Across 422 studies, mostly on health and social behaviors rather than buying, intentions accounted for 28% of the variance in later behavior on average (Sheeran, 2002): a large effect by research standards, yet most of what people did went unexplained. With enough interviews, you can even [code them into variables and test them with logistic regression](/insights/interviews-and-logistic-regression).

## How do you write an insight statement?

I write each insight as one sentence in three parts. I call it the Do–Because–So template:

> People [do something you can observe] because [the tension or motive behind it], so [what it means for the decision].

The middle part carries the weight. A tension is two things a person wants that pull against each other, like trying a product and not making a money decision alone. If you can't name the tension, you probably have a finding, not yet an insight.

Another hypothetical market insight example, weak version first:

- Weak: "Customers value reliability."
- Insight: "Households book a cleaner once and don't rebook because a different stranger each time feels risky, so sending the same cleaner may matter more than the price."

The weak version isn't wrong. It names a preference without a motive and leaves the decision where it was.

## How do you tell a real insight from a restated fact?

Before an insight goes into a deck, it has to pass four tests. Fail one and it goes back:

1. **The why test.** Does it name a motive or tension, or only restate what happened? "Churn peaks in month two" fails.
2. **The surprise test.** Would a sharp colleague have predicted it without the research? If everyone nods, it may be true, but it isn't new. This is Laughlin's "non-obvious," and in my experience the hardest test to admit failing.
3. **The two-source test.** Do two different kinds of evidence point the same way, ideally one about what people say and one about what they do?
4. **The decision test.** If it's true, what will you do differently, and what result would show it's wrong? If nothing would change, it's trivia.

Laughlin's definition adds a guardrail: mutual benefit. An insight that only pays off by exploiting a customer's weakness isn't one I'll act on, and I check the changes it suggests with [a five-question test for dark patterns](/insights/persuasion-or-dark-pattern-test). Even then, it's a hypothesis until the decision it suggests has been tested.

## How I turn interviews and benchmarks into insights

For [Musicity](/work/musicity), a music platform with three groups of users (artists, listeners and venues), the decision was which group to win first. I assessed demand through semi-structured interviews with each group, coded them into jobs to be done (Christensen et al., 2016) and ranked the jobs by the strength of the evidence, weighting what people had done above what they said they wanted. That ranked view of demand was the basis for my recommendation on which segment to win first, its positioning and the launch order. The findings belong to the client; the method is in [jobs-to-be-done interviews for market fit](/insights/jobs-to-be-done-market-fit-interviews).

For [RecallMate](/work/recallmate), an AI-assisted learning app I worked on as a research intern at the University of Milano-Bicocca, I benchmarked study methods against Anki and Quizlet: Anki's scheduling is mature, but setup and card-writing take effort; Quizlet is easy to start but less rigorous about long-term review. With multi-round interviews, that led to a working insight: learners drop flashcard tools before the benefit shows because the effort comes first, so the first version should make card creation easy, with AI help, while recall stays with the learner. It was a prototype, so the insight stayed a hypothesis, but it shaped the MoSCoW priorities: AI-assisted card creation became a must-have.

## Where to start with your own data

Pick one decision your team has to make this quarter and collect what you already have on it: the dashboard, the last survey, a week of support tickets. Write the best insight you can with the Do–Because–So template, then run the four tests. Where it fails tells you what to do next. Fail the why test and you need interviews; fail the two-source test, a second kind of evidence; fail the surprise test, deeper digging; fail the decision test, and you're answering the wrong question.
