People become game creators for the same reasons people write, draw or make music: to say something, to get good at something and to share it with others. Money is rarely the first reason. Self-expression, mastery, giving, recognition, belonging and the plain fun of tinkering come first, and the platforms that keep creators making are the ones that feed those motives instead of replacing them.

I'm an applied psychologist, and I'm designing [Pukytoon](/work/pukytoon), a feed of games anyone can make, which is still in development. Understanding why people make games is most of my job right now. Here's what the research says, what I've seen, and what it means for anyone building tools or platforms for game creators.

## What are the six reasons people make games?

From the research and from watching people make things, I group the motives into six. Most makers have several at once.

| Motive | What the maker wants | What the psychology says | What a platform should give |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Express | To say something: an idea, a feeling, a joke | Self-expression meets the need for autonomy (Ryan & Deci, 2000) | Freedom to choose the theme, look and rules |
| Master | To get good at making | Competence is a basic need, and belief in your own ability grows with small successes (Bandura, 1977) | A first game that works on the first try, then room to grow |
| Give | To make something for someone | Relatedness, the third basic need | One link to send a game to the person it's for |
| Be seen | To be recognized for what they made | Recognition and status motivate, and visible approval guides others (Cialdini, 1984) | The maker's name on every game, likes, followers |
| Belong | To be part of a group of makers | Participatory cultures give members a sense that their contributions matter (Jenkins and colleagues, 2006) | Remixing, community spaces, mentorship |
| Tinker | To play with how a system works | People learn by making things (Papert, 1980) | A live preview that answers every change |

## Why is making a game so satisfying?

Self-determination theory explains a lot of it (Ryan & Deci, 2000). People need autonomy, competence and relatedness, and making a game can meet all three at once: the choices are yours, the game works, and someone plays it.

Making also gives a particular kind of absorption. Mihaly Csikszentmihalyi (1990) described flow as most likely when the challenge matches your skill, with clear goals and immediate feedback. Tuning a game while a preview plays is close to a textbook setup: you change something, you see the result straight away, and the next change is just within reach.

Then there's ownership. Norton, Mochon and Ariely (2012) found that people value what they've built themselves, the IKEA effect. A maker's own game is worth more to them than a better game they only played, which is why a first game, however simple, can be the start of a habit.

## Is making games a way of learning?

Yes, and that's one of the oldest findings in this area. Seymour Papert, from Mindstorms (1980) on, argued that people learn especially well when they build things they care about, an idea later named constructionism. Yasmin Kafai (1995) showed that designing games can be a rich context for children's learning.

What I find striking is that the learning hides inside the fun. Someone tuning the speed of an endless runner is reasoning about difficulty curves. Someone writing a rule like score ≥ 1000 is writing a condition, one of the basic building blocks of programming, without calling it that. That's another reason to make the first steps of game-making as easy as possible: the learning starts with them.

## What keeps game creators making?

The hardest game to make is the second one. The first comes from curiosity; the second needs a reason. In my view, three things decide whether it gets made.

The first is an audience. If nobody plays the first game, the second rarely happens. Likes, plays and especially people making their own version of a game tell a maker that someone cared, and following makers turns that into an audience that waits.

The second is momentum. Each game should be easier to start than the last: a saved look, a rule you can reuse, a remix of your own earlier game. People are more likely to keep going when they can see progress already made (Nunes & Drèze, 2006).

The third is credit. When others build on your work, credit is what you get back. In a study of the Scratch community, Andrés Monroy-Hernández and colleagues (2011) found that credit remixers gave in their own words went with friendlier reactions, while credit the system added automatically didn't have that effect. Makers notice when they're thanked, and when they aren't.

## Should game creators be paid?

Money is a fair question, and the psychology warns against the obvious answer. When people do something for the joy of it, expected rewards for doing it can turn play into work. Lepper, Greene and Nisbett (1973) found that children who expected a reward for drawing later drew less in their free time than children who hadn't been promised one. A meta-analysis found that expected tangible rewards tend to undermine intrinsic motivation, while positive, informational feedback tends to support it (Deci, Koestner & Ryan, 1999).

That doesn't mean creators shouldn't earn. It means paying per game published is the wrong design: it rewards volume and teaches makers that the game is a chore. Recognition first, and payment designed to reward quality and audience rather than output, is the safer path. I cover the broader pattern in [gamification that doesn't backfire](/insights/gamification-overjustification-edtech).

## Why do game creators quit?

The reasons mirror the motives. Makers quit when nobody plays their games, so there's no one to make for. They quit when feedback is cruel, or when there's none at all. They quit when their work is copied without credit. And they burn out when making turns into chasing an algorithm instead of making something they care about.

Every one of these is a design problem as much as a personal one. A platform decides whether new games get a first audience, whether credit is visible, whether cruelty can be reported and hidden, and whether its rewards feed the joy of making or replace it.
