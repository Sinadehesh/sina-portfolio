Customers hesitate to buy because a doubt goes unanswered at the exact moment it appears. Not because the button is the wrong color, and usually not because they don't want the thing. In my work, almost every drop-off I have investigated traces back to one of four doubts — and once you can name which one, the fix is usually obvious.

I call this the Four Gates model. Every decision to buy, book or sign up passes through four gates, in order: **Understand**, **Trust**, **Try** and **Commit**. At each gate a single doubt can turn a person around. Below is the model, how to find the gate that is leaking, and the fix that belongs at each one.

## What are the four gates of a decision?

**Gate 1 — Understand.** The doubt: *"What is this, exactly?"* The barrier is uncertainty. When people cannot tell within a few seconds what you offer and whether it is for them, they leave — not because they decided no, but because deciding at all started to feel like work. The fix is one clear promise: what it is, who it is for and what they get, in a single line.

**Gate 2 — Trust.** The doubt: *"Can I trust them?"* The barrier is low trust, and it is steepest for brands nobody has heard of. People look for evidence that others have taken this step and been fine — what Cialdini (1984) called social proof — and for signs of competence and responsiveness. The fix is proof, placed where the doubt arises.

**Gate 3 — Try.** The doubt: *"Looks like work."* The barrier is effort: long forms, mandatory accounts, a setup that has to happen before anything useful does. Fogg (2009) describes behavior as the meeting of motivation, ability and a prompt; when a step feels hard, teams tend to pump motivation with more copy, when the better move is to make the step smaller. The fix is a shorter path.

**Gate 4 — Commit.** The doubt: *"What if it goes wrong?"* The barrier is perceived risk. Losses loom larger than equivalent gains (Kahneman & Tversky, 1979), so the possible downside of a purchase weighs more than its promised upside. The fix is risk reversal: a free first step, a trial, an easy cancellation or a guarantee that moves the risk from the customer to you.

## Why does the order of the gates matter?

The gates are sequential. Someone lost at Understand never sees your guarantee, so a fix at a later gate cannot recover them. Yet the most common thing I see teams do is jump straight to Commit — discounts, countdowns, money-back badges — when the real leak is that nobody understood the offer.

Losses also compound. As a purely illustrative example: if each gate kept seven in ten visitors, about a quarter of the people who arrived would make it through all four. Raise only the first gate to nine in ten and roughly three in ten make it — without touching anything else. The earliest gate with a real leak is almost always the cheapest place to win.

## How do you find out which gate is losing people?

Analytics tells you **where** people leave. It cannot tell you **why**. You need both, and the diagnosis takes days, not months:

1. **Map your funnel to the gates.** Your hero section is mostly Understand; pricing and reviews are Trust; the form or setup is Try; the checkout, contract or final button is Commit.
2. **Find the steepest drop.** Look at step-to-step completion, not just the final conversion rate.
3. **Collect the doubt in the customer's own words.** Five to eight short interviews with people who almost bought, a one-question survey on the page ("What's stopping you today?"), and a handful of session recordings are usually enough to hear a pattern.
4. **Code the doubts into the four barriers.** This is a light version of thematic analysis (Braun & Clarke, 2006): group what people said, then label each group as uncertainty, low trust, effort or perceived risk.
5. **Fix the earliest leaking gate first**, then measure the same step again.

Here is the cheat sheet I use to match symptoms to gates:

| Gate | What you see in the data | What people say | First fix to test |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Understand | High bounce, short time on the first screen | "I wasn't sure what it was" | Rewrite the headline as one promise |
| Trust | Visits to reviews or About, then exit | "I'd never heard of them" | Put proof next to the call to action |
| Try | Form starts but few completions | "It asked for too much" | Cut fields, delay the account |
| Commit | Checkout reached, not finished | "What if it doesn't work?" | Add a risk-reversing first step |

## How I used the Four Gates on a B2B website

[Optimolds](/work/optimolds) is an Italy-based composite engineering and mold-making firm, and its buyers are risk-averse for a good reason: a bad mold ruins a production run. When I designed and built its trilingual website and lead-generation funnel, each gate got a deliberate answer.

- **Understand:** the headline states the whole offer in one line — from CAD file to finished composite part.
- **Trust:** a trust bar sits directly under the call to action, with reviews, reply time, accepted CAD formats and location.
- **Try:** instead of a long project brief, a short CAD-upload form. The file tells the engineer more than ten questions would.
- **Commit:** a free 48-hour feasibility check as the single primary call to action across the site — the buyer risks nothing to find out whether the part can be made.

I wrote up the last of these in more depth in [why a free check beats a contact form](/insights/risk-reversal-b2b-lead-generation).

## Where should each fix go?

The answer to a doubt only works if it is visible at the moment the doubt happens. A wall of client logos on the About page does almost nothing for the person hesitating over a form two pages away. Proof belongs next to the price, reassurance next to the form, and the guarantee next to the button that commits.

This is why I treat placement as part of the fix, not decoration. It is also why [trust signals for an unknown brand](/insights/trust-signals-unknown-brand) are more about position than quantity.

## What the Four Gates model is not

It is not a license to manipulate. Fixing a gate means answering a real doubt truthfully. If the honest answer to "What if it goes wrong?" is uncomfortable, the fix is in the product or the terms, not in the copy. Before I ship any persuasive change I run it through [a five-question test for dark patterns](/insights/persuasion-or-dark-pattern-test).

It is also not a substitute for research. The model is a way to organize what you learn from customers so it turns into decisions — it does not tell you what your customers doubt. Only they can.

## Try it on your own funnel this week

Open your funnel and walk it as a hesitant customer. At every step, write down the doubt you would have, in plain words. Mark which gate it belongs to. Then check whether the answer to that doubt is visible on that screen, at that moment. The first place where it isn't is your next experiment.

The same exercise works far beyond websites — I have applied it to [insurance quote forms](/insights/insurance-quote-funnel-conversion), onboarding flows and commitment apps.
