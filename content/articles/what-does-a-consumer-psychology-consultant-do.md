A consumer psychology consultant works out why customers hesitate, choose or leave, and then changes the offer, the message or the product so the decision gets easier. The work is grounded in evidence about how real buyers decide, and it ends with a measured change in what they do, not with a slide deck.

I'm a consumer psychology consultant based in Milan. Below is the role as I practice it: the steps, how it differs from the agencies and specialists you may already use, the signs you need one, and what a first engagement looks like.

## What does a consumer psychology consultant actually do?

I treat a conversion problem as a decision problem. Somewhere between "this looks interesting" and "yes", a doubt appears and goes unanswered. My work is to find that doubt, prove it's the real one, and remove it.

In practice that runs in five steps:

1. **Diagnose.** Audit the funnel, the analytics and the customer touchpoints to find where people stop.
2. **Research.** Interviews, short on-page surveys or quick experiments to find out why they stop, in their own words.
3. **Redesign.** Change the offer, the message or the flow around the barrier the research confirmed, not the one the team assumed.
4. **Build.** Ship the website, funnel or product change directly, so nothing gets lost in a hand-off.
5. **Measure.** Instrument the change and read the result against the original hypothesis.

The barriers are usually the same few: uncertainty about what you offer, low trust, too much effort, perceived risk, and sometimes simply too many options. I organize them with my [Four Gates model of why customers hesitate](/insights/four-gates-why-customers-hesitate), which pairs each barrier with the fix that belongs to it.

## How is it different from a marketing agency, a UX researcher or a CRO specialist?

All of these roles are valuable, and the lines blur in practice. The clearest difference is the starting question:

| Role | Starting question | Typical output | Where it usually stops |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Marketing agency | How do we reach more of the right people? | Campaigns, creative, media plans | At the click that brings people in |
| UX researcher | Can people use this, and what do they need? | Findings, personas, usability reports | At recommendations for the product team |
| CRO specialist | Which version of this page converts better? | A/B tests on existing traffic | At what a test on current traffic can show |
| Consumer psychology consultant | What doubt stops this buyer, and what answers it? | Diagnosis, redesigned offer and flow, measured change | At the behavior change, or the evidence it didn't happen |

That's my opinion, not an industry standard. Good CRO specialists do research, and good agencies think hard about buyers. But the starting question shapes the answer. If you start from a page, you test pages. If you start from a decision, you sometimes find the page was never the problem: the first step cost too much, or the offer asked for trust it hadn't earned.

My own angle is the combination of consumer psychology, research methods and building the thing myself. The person who ran the interviews is the person who ships the change and reads the numbers.

## Is a consumer psychology consultant a psychologist?

Not necessarily, and the words matter. In Italy, "psychologist" is a protected professional title, so the accurate name for what I do is consumer psychology consultant. My training is an M.Sc. in Applied Experimental Psychological Sciences from the University of Milano-Bicocca, with a focus on consumer psychology, psychometrics and human-technology interaction. I don't assess or treat people.

The unit I work on is a decision in a market: a buyer choosing a supplier, a visitor deciding whether to fill in a form, a user deciding whether to come back tomorrow. If you need support with someone's wellbeing, you need a psychologist. If you need to know why people abandon your quote form, that's my job.

## When should you hire a consumer psychology consultant?

I look for five symptoms. One is enough to start a conversation; three usually means the problem sits in the decision, not in the traffic.

- People arrive but don't act. Traffic is healthy, the button has been recolored twice, and the form stays empty.
- Interest goes quiet. Buyers say "interesting", download the brochure and never reply.
- Your tests come back flat. Variant after variant, no difference, which often means you're polishing the wrong step.
- A big bet rests on opinions. You're about to build a feature or enter a segment, and the evidence is what the team believes.
- People sign up and vanish. The first purchase happens; the habit doesn't.

There are also two signs you don't need one yet. If almost nobody visits, you have a reach problem, and a marketing agency will serve you better. If the product doesn't do what it promises, no amount of choice architecture will fix it, and I'd tell you so.

## What does a first engagement look like?

Usually a funnel psychology audit. It's a contained piece of work with a clear output, and it turns a vague "conversion is low" into a ranked list of specific problems.

I walk the funnel as a hesitant customer, step by step, and match each drop-off to the barrier behind it: perceived risk, effort, uncertainty or choice overload. Then I check that guess against evidence: analytics for where, and a handful of conversations, recordings or one-question surveys for why.

Every finding ends up on what I call an audit card. Here is a hypothetical one for a quote form:

- **Where:** step two of the quote form, with many starts and few finishes.
- **What people say:** "I didn't know what would happen after I pressed send."
- **Barrier:** uncertainty at the moment of commitment.
- **Change:** one line under the button saying who replies, how and by when.
- **Measure:** step-two completion from the same traffic sources, before and after.

The cards are ranked by how early the leak sits and how cheap the fix is. You can implement them yourself, or I build them. I prefer the second, because nothing gets lost translating research into a brief.

## What does this look like in real projects?

Three projects show the range.

[Optimolds](/work/optimolds), an Italy-based composite engineering and mold-making firm, sells to risk-averse buyers: a bad mold ruins a production run. I designed and built its trilingual website and lead-generation funnel around that fear, with a free 48-hour feasibility check as risk reversal, a short CAD-upload form to cut effort, and trust signals next to one clear call to action. I explain the reasoning in [why a free check beats a contact form](/insights/risk-reversal-b2b-lead-generation).

For Musicity, a music platform with three segments (artists, listeners and venues), the question was strategic: who to win first. I assessed market fit through interviews across all three, coded them into a ranked jobs-to-be-done view, and recommended which segment to lead with, how to position for it and the launch order. The method is in [jobs-to-be-done interviews for market fit](/insights/jobs-to-be-done-market-fit-interviews).

OopsCupid is my own product, a live relationship-assessment platform with 15 free research-informed tests. Its key decision was psychological: I removed sign-up entirely, trading email capture for completion, because a form before the first result is exactly the kind of effort that makes people leave.

## Working with a consumer psychology consultant in Milan, Italy or remotely

I'm based in Milan and work with teams in Milan, Turin, across Europe and remotely, in English, Italian, Persian and German. Language matters more than it sounds: the Optimolds site runs in English, Italian and German because buyers across Europe evaluate a supplier in their own language.

Most of the work runs well online. Being in the same city helps for workshops and for meeting customers where they actually buy. You can see the services and how an engagement runs on my [consumer psychology consulting](/consumer-psychology) page.

Whoever you hire, ask one question before you sign: "What will you measure, and what result would tell us you were wrong?" A consultant who can answer that in a sentence is treating your funnel as a hypothesis to test, not a canvas to decorate. That answer is most of the job.
