Engagement is coming back to something because it adds to your life, and being able to stop whenever you want. Addiction is coming back after you've lost control, while the activity takes priority over things you value and you keep going despite the harm. From the outside the two can look the same: lots of time, lots of returns. The research shows they're different on the inside, and that design can push people toward one or the other.

I'm an applied psychologist, and I design apps and games that I want people to love using. That makes this distinction the most important one in my work. Here's what the research says, the warning signs, and the test I use. This is about design and everyday judgment, not a clinical diagnosis.

## What is the difference between engagement and addiction?

The clearest definitions come from medicine. The World Health Organization's ICD-11, adopted in 2019 and in effect since January 1, 2022, describes gaming disorder as impaired control over gaming, increasing priority given to gaming over other interests and daily activities, and continuing or escalating despite negative consequences. The pattern has to cause significant impairment in personal, family, social, educational or work life, and normally be evident over at least 12 months. In the American Psychiatric Association's DSM-5 (2013), gambling disorder is the only behavioral addiction in the chapter on substance-related and addictive disorders, and internet gaming disorder is listed as a condition for further study.

Mark Griffiths (2005) described addiction through six components: salience, mood modification, tolerance, withdrawal, conflict and relapse. The trouble is that some of these also describe people who simply love something. That's where the most useful study in this area comes in.

## Which signs point to addiction, and which to high engagement?

John Charlton and Ian Danforth (2007) surveyed 442 players of the online game Asheron's Call and found that the classic addiction criteria split into two groups. Some reflected high engagement: thinking about the game a lot, needing more of it over time and feeling euphoria while playing. Others reflected addiction: conflict with other parts of life, withdrawal symptoms, relapse after trying to cut down, and the game taking over daily behavior.

| Sign | In plain words | What it reflected in the study |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Cognitive salience | Thinking about it often | High engagement |
| Tolerance | Wanting more of it over time | High engagement |
| Euphoria | A buzz while doing it | High engagement |
| Conflict | Clashes with work, school or people | Addiction |
| Withdrawal | Feeling irritable or low when you can't do it | Addiction |
| Relapse | Going back to it after deciding to stop | Addiction |
| Behavioral salience | It taking over what you actually do each day | Addiction |

In other words, loving something isn't the problem. Losing control of it, and paying for it in the rest of your life, is.

## Harmonious and obsessive passion

Robert Vallerand and colleagues (2003) drew a related line between two kinds of passion. Harmonious passion is freely chosen and fits alongside the rest of your life. Obsessive passion feels like an internal pressure: you do the activity because you feel you have to, and it crowds out other things.

Andrew Przybylski and colleagues (2009) applied this to video games. Players whose basic psychological needs weren't being met in daily life showed more obsessive passion: they played more, felt more tension after playing and enjoyed games less. Heavy play predicted lower well-being only among obsessively passionate players. The same game, played the same number of hours, was healthy for one person and harmful for another.

## Why do some people slide into compulsion?

Part of the answer is what an activity gives people. Richard Ryan, Scott Rigby and Andrew Przybylski (2006) found that games which made players feel autonomous and competent were enjoyed more and chosen again, and in online multiplayer play, relatedness mattered too. These are the same three needs of self-determination theory (Ryan & Deci, 2000). Meeting them is what good engagement is made of.

The other part is what people bring. Daniel Kardefelt-Winther (2014) argued that much problematic internet use is compensatory: people go online to escape problems or relieve bad moods, so heavy use can be a symptom of something else as much as a cause. That's why blaming the activity alone often misses the point.

## Is screen time the problem?

Not on its own. Amy Orben and Andrew Przybylski (2019) analyzed three large datasets of adolescents and found the association between digital technology use and well-being to be negative but very small, explaining less than half of one percent of the differences in well-being, about the same as the association with regularly eating potatoes. In a study of more than 120,000 English adolescents, Przybylski and Netta Weinstein (2017) concluded that moderate use of digital technology is not intrinsically harmful and may be advantageous. Both studies are correlational, so they can't prove cause, but they agree that minutes are a poor measure. Control, what the time replaces and how people feel afterward say much more.

## Which design choices tip engagement into compulsion?

The difference between an engaging product and a compulsive one is often a handful of design choices. These are the ones I watch for:

- Rewards on an unpredictable schedule, especially when money is involved (Ferster & Skinner, 1957).
- Near-misses and losses celebrated like wins, borrowed from slot machines, which I cover in [the dangers of gambling](/insights/dangers-of-gambling-governments).
- Feeds and sessions with no natural end.
- Streaks that wipe out a lot of progress after one missed day.
- Artificial timers and scarcity that create pressure to return.
- Dark patterns that make leaving, canceling or saying no harder than saying yes (Brignull, 2010; Mathur and colleagues, 2019).

Nir Eyal's Hooked (2014) set out the trigger, action, variable reward and investment loop that many products follow. Five years later his book Indistractable (2019) was about helping people control their attention. The tools are the same; what matters is whose interests they serve.

## The healthy engagement test

This is the test I use on any product, including my own. Six questions, each with a healthy sign and a warning sign:

| Question | Healthy sign | Warning sign |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Would people come back without reminders? | Yes, because they enjoy it | Only when pinged, nagged or rewarded for returning |
| Does it end? | Sessions, stories and programs have natural endings | It's designed never to run out |
| Can people leave at no cost? | A break loses nothing important | A missed day destroys progress or status |
| Is money clear and chosen? | Prices are in real money and nothing random is sold | Random rewards for money, hidden prices, pressure to pay |
| How do people feel afterward? | Better, or at least no worse | Tense, guilty or drained |
| What does it replace? | Other leisure | Sleep, work, school or people |

A product doesn't need to be perfect on every line. But if it fails several, it's probably earning its engagement the wrong way.

## What do designers owe the people who use their products?

Designers can measure the right things: not only minutes and return visits, but whether people feel in control and better off, which I discuss in [measuring behavior change](/insights/measuring-behavior-change-apps). They can build endings and exits as carefully as they build hooks. In [DeBubble](/work/debubble), a self-care app I designed, progress only goes up, with no streaks to lose. In the [app lockers](/work/app-lockers) I designed, friction against mindless scrolling comes with escape hatches, so it helps without trapping anyone.

I explain how I apply this test to my own apps and games in [engaging, not addictive](/insights/engaging-not-addictive-design). The goal isn't less engagement. It's engagement people would choose again if they could see exactly how it works.
