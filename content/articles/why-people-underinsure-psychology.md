People under-insure because insurance asks them to pay a certain cost today to protect against a loss that is uncertain, distant and hard to picture — and that is exactly the kind of trade the human mind discounts. Much of the protection gap is not ignorance or stinginess. It is predictable psychology, and predictable psychology can be designed for.

I have never worked for an insurer. I come at this as a consumer psychology consultant who designs funnels and decision flows, and insurance is one of the hardest decisions I know: paying for something you hope never to use. Below are the biases I think explain most of the gap, the research behind them, and what an insurer could do about each one without crossing into manipulation.

## Why don't people buy insurance they need?

Start with the shape of the decision. The premium is certain, immediate and visible on a bank statement. The benefit is a maybe, years away, tied to an event nobody wants to imagine. Every part of that sentence works against the purchase.

Present bias is the first force. We weight the present far more heavily than the future, even when we know better (Laibson, 1997; O'Donoghue & Rabin, 1999). "I'll sort out cover next month" is not a decision against insurance. It is a decision that never quite becomes urgent. Savings apps fight the same force, which I wrote about in [present bias and designing for the future you](/insights/present-bias-savings-app-design); insurance is its close cousin.

The second force is quieter. Many of us carry a background sense that serious losses happen to other people. A policy asks us to argue with that feeling, and few people enjoy the argument.

## How do people misjudge small probabilities?

Insurance lives in the tail of the distribution, which is where judgment is weakest. Kahneman and Tversky (1979) noted that highly unlikely events tend to be either ignored or overweighted. The same flood risk can register as zero for one homeowner and as a nightmare for the next.

Sunstein (2002) described probability neglect: when a risk stirs strong emotion, people respond to how bad the outcome would be and largely stop attending to how likely it is. The flip side matters just as much for insurance. A risk that stirs no emotion at all, like a slow leak behind a wall or a long illness, barely registers.

Vividness decides which side a risk lands on. Johnson, Hershey, Meszaros and Kunreuther (1993) found that people were willing to pay more for flight insurance covering terrorism than for a policy covering death from any cause, which of course includes terrorism. Slovic's (1987) work on risk perception points the same way: dread and unfamiliarity shape how risky something feels, often more than the statistics do.

The uncomfortable conclusion is that people don't buy insurance in proportion to risk. They buy it in proportion to how easily they can picture the loss.

## The quiet-year problem: why cover lapses

The protection gap is not only about people who never buy. It is also about people who buy and then let go. Kunreuther, Pauly and McMorrow (2013) describe a recurring pattern: cover is often bought after a disaster rather than before one, and dropped after a few uneventful years, as if premiums that produced no claim were money wasted.

Mental accounting explains why that feels reasonable from the inside. Thaler (1985, 1999) showed that people keep informal accounts for their money and judge each one on its own. In the "insurance account", years of premiums with no payout look like a loss, even though the policy did exactly what it promised: it carried the risk.

The certainty effect adds a twist. People place a special value on outcomes that are certain (Kahneman & Tversky, 1979), so cover that seems to remove a risk completely feels worth much more than cover that merely reduces it. When an excess or an exclusion surfaces at claim time, the promise of certainty breaks, and trust goes with it.

## The protection-gap map: five biases, five honest fixes

Here is the map I use. Each row pairs a bias with a fix that answers the real doubt, and with the manipulative twin that answers it dishonestly. The twin often works in the short term, which is exactly why it needs a name.

| Bias | What it sounds like | Honest fix | Manipulative twin |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Present bias | "I'll sort it out later" | Offer cover when the risk changes, in one short step | Countdown timers and fake deadlines |
| Probability neglect | "It won't happen to me" | One concrete scenario: what happens, what it costs, what the policy pays | Disaster imagery that inflates fear |
| Vividness | "I only need cover for that" | Show broad cover next to the narrow cover it includes | Premium-priced narrow cover for vivid risks |
| Certainty effect | "So I'm fully covered?" | State the excess and the main exclusion as plainly as the cover | "Total peace of mind" copy that hides the limits |
| Mental accounting | "I paid for nothing" | A yearly note on what the policy carried for you | Guilt messages when people try to leave |

## Defaults at the right moment

Defaults are among the most powerful tools in choice architecture. Johnson and Goldstein (2003) showed how much organ-donor registration depends on whether a country asks people to opt in or opt out, and Madrian and Shea (2001) found that automatic enrollment transformed participation in retirement saving, with many people staying wherever the default put them. Insurance has its own version: in a natural experiment described by Johnson and colleagues (1993), drivers in two neighboring US states mostly kept whichever car-insurance option their state had made the default.

Two cautions. First, timing. A default works best when exposure genuinely changes, such as buying a home, having a child or signing a lease, because the risk is real and the person is already deciding. Second, the law. In the EU, the Consumer Rights Directive requires express consent for payments beyond the main purchase, and a pre-ticked box doesn't count. So for a paid add-on, the honest default is a recommended option the customer still chooses, never a box ticked for them. I go deeper on that in [embedded insurance at checkout](/insights/embedded-insurance-checkout-design). This is not legal advice; check the rules that apply to your product.

## What building a commitment app taught me about insurance

[Costly](/work/costly), the commitment app I'm building, is in some ways the mirror image of insurance. Insurance asks people to pay a small, certain cost now against a large, uncertain one later. Costly makes a vague future cost, a lost evening of doomscrolling, felt now: as money, at an hourly rate the user sets for their own time.

The lesson I'd carry across is that meaning beats amount. Costly frames losses against a five-tier ladder of things the user actually wants, because a bare number is forgettable. Insurance communication often does the opposite: it shows the premium as a bare number and the loss as a vague category. I'd flip that. Describe the uninsured loss in the customer's own terms, such as months of rent or a replaced laptop, and keep the premium plain and per period.

I haven't tested these fixes with an insurer, so treat them as hypotheses to run, not results. The first place I'd test them is the quote itself, which I break down in [where insurance quote funnels lose people](/insights/insurance-quote-funnel-conversion).

If you work on an insurance product, pick the row of the map that best matches your drop-off and lapse data, and build its honest fix first. The protection gap won't close with louder fear. It closes when the risk is easy to picture, the decision arrives at the right moment, and the policy keeps showing its value in the years when nothing goes wrong.
