A consumer psychologist studies how people perceive, choose, buy and use products and services, and turns that evidence into decisions for a business, a nonprofit, a public body or a research program. In practice that means designing studies, running experiments, interviews and surveys, analyzing what people did and why, and explaining what should change as a result.

The work happens in universities, research agencies, brands, product teams, public bodies and consultancies, often under a job title without the word "psychologist" in it. I'm an example: I do this kind of work, but I'm not a psychologist, for reasons I explain below.

If you're new to the field itself, start with [what consumer psychology is](/insights/what-is-consumer-psychology). This article is about the job.

## What does a consumer psychologist actually do?

As I see it, an applied project runs through six steps. If you're writing or reading a consumer psychologist job description, these are the responsibilities it should cover:

1. Turn a business or policy question into a research question. "Why are sales flat?" becomes "At which step do interested buyers stop, and what are they unsure about there?"
2. Choose the method: an experiment, a survey, an interview study, an analysis of behavioral data, or a mix.
3. Collect the data, or brief and supervise the agency that does.
4. Analyze it: statistics for the numbers, systematic coding for the words.
5. Turn the findings into a recommendation, with the evidence and its limits stated plainly.
6. Check afterwards whether the decision changed what people actually did.

The APA Dictionary of Psychology puts the core in one sentence: "Consumer psychologists investigate the psychological processes underlying consumer behavior and responses to both for-profit and not-for-profit marketing." The skills underneath are research design, statistics, psychometrics, interviewing and clear writing. In my view the last one is the most underrated, because a finding nobody acts on hasn't done its job.

## Where do consumer psychologists work?

Mainly in academia and industry, with public bodies, nonprofits and consulting alongside. Pham (2013) notes that "the vast majority of academic consumer psychologists work in business schools rather than in social science departments." For industry, a 2008 careers interview in The Psychologist, the British Psychological Society's magazine, described graduates going "into market research, advertising and marketing companies and marketing departments".

Here is how the settings compare:

| Setting | Typical titles | What they do | What they deliver |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| University, usually a business school | Professor or lecturer in marketing, researcher | Experiments and field studies on how people judge and choose; teaching | Papers, courses, supervised theses |
| Market research agency | Researcher, research manager | Surveys, interviews, concept and ad tests for client brands | Study reports, segmentations, recommendations |
| In-house insights team | Consumer insights manager or analyst | Keeps the company's picture of its customers current; commissions research | Customer profiles, tracking studies, input to plans |
| Advertising and communications | Strategist, planner, research lead | Works out what an audience believes and which message could move it | Creative briefs, message tests |
| Product, UX and pricing teams | UX researcher, behavioral scientist | Tests flows, defaults, prices and onboarding with real users | Usability findings, experiment results, design decisions |
| Public bodies and nonprofits | Behavioral scientist, researcher, evaluator | Applies the same methods to campaigns, labels and services | Trials, evaluations, policy recommendations |
| Consulting | Consumer insights or consumer psychology consultant | Diagnoses one client's decision problem and tests the fix | A diagnosis, the change, a measured result |

Treat the titles as examples, not a standard. The same interview noted that jobs titled "Consumer Psychologist" are rare; such people "tend to work in communications and analysis in both profit and not-for-profit organisations, often in marketing or research departments". The product and UX row comes from my own experience rather than a published source. It's where a lot of my own work sits.

## What questions do consumer psychologists get asked?

A realistic list, in the words clients tend to use, with how each is usually answered:

- "Why do interested people stop before they buy or get in touch?" Funnel data for where, interviews or one-question surveys for why.
- "Which of these three names, packs or messages should we launch?" A controlled comparison with the target audience, not a vote in the office.
- "If we raise the price, who leaves?" Past purchase data, a carefully designed pricing study or a small live test.
- "Which customers should we go after first?" Interviews across segments, ranked by evidence of real demand.
- "Do people understand this label, contract or form?" Comprehension tests with the people who will actually read it.
- "Why did the campaign get attention but no sales?" Separating whether people noticed it, believed it and could act on it.
- "Is this persuasive or manipulative?" A review against a clear ethical test, before launch.

None of these is "tell us about our customers". Useful questions name a decision. When a client can't name one yet, finding it is the first piece of work.

## Is "consumer psychologist" a protected title?

In some countries it effectively is, because the word "psychologist" is protected, and that shapes who does this work. My own position first: I'm not a psychologist. In Italy the title is protected, so I work as a behavioral scientist and consumer psychology consultant. I trained in experimental psychology, but I'm not registered with the Ordine degli Psicologi and I don't use the title.

What the rules say in Italy, the UK and the US:

- Italy: only psychologists enrolled in Section A of the Albo of the Ordine degli Psicologi may use the title "psicologo" or practice the profession (L. 56/1989, art. 2; DPR 328/2001, art. 50). Using the title without enrollment falls under art. 498 of the penal code and carries an administrative fine; practicing the profession without the qualification is a separate crime under art. 348.
- UK: the Health and Care Professions Council (HCPC) states that "The job title 'psychologist', is not, by itself, protected by law." Nine titles are, including "practitioner psychologist", "registered psychologist" and "clinical psychologist", and using one while not on the HCPC Register "is a criminal offence". "Consumer psychologist" is not among them.
- US: psychologists are licensed by the states. The Association of State and Provincial Psychology Boards reports that "the prevailing standard is that a psychologist must have obtained a doctoral degree in psychology" to practice independently (ASPPB, 2024). The model licensing acts treat anyone using a title with "psychology, psychological, or psychologist" as holding themselves out as a psychologist, with exemptions for people engaged solely in academic teaching or research. State laws vary, so don't assume "consumer psychologist" is free to use.

The practical result is that much consumer psychology work is done by consumer insights managers, behavioral scientists, UX researchers and consultants. If you want to build that career, I lay out the routes in [how to become a consumer psychologist](/insights/how-to-become-a-consumer-psychologist). This is general information, not legal advice, and rules change, so check with the national regulator before relying on any of it.

## How is a consumer psychologist different from a consumer psychology consultant?

The methods overlap; the title and the arrangement differ. Where "psychologist" is regulated, the title tells you someone is registered. "Consultant" tells you how they work: brought in from outside for a defined problem, and accountable for a recommendation or a change rather than an ongoing research program.

Whichever you hire, I'd judge them on the same three things: what they trained in, the method they'd use on your question, and what they'll measure to know whether it worked. I describe the consultant's side in [what a consumer psychology consultant does](/insights/what-does-a-consumer-psychology-consultant-do).

## What does this work look like in practice?

Two of my recent projects match two questions from the list above.

For [Optimolds](/work/optimolds), an Italy-based composite engineering and mold-making firm, the question was why a cautious engineering buyer wouldn't contact a supplier they don't know. A bad mold ruins a production run, so the barrier is perceived risk. I designed and built the trilingual website and lead-generation funnel around it: a free 48-hour feasibility check as risk reversal, a short CAD-upload form to cut effort, and trust signals next to one clear call to action.

For [Musicity](/work/musicity), a music platform, the question was which customers to go after first. I assessed market fit through interviews with artists, listeners and venues, coded them into a ranked jobs-to-be-done view of demand, and recommended which segment to win first, how to position for it and the launch order. The choice stays with the client; the method is in [jobs-to-be-done interviews for market fit](/insights/jobs-to-be-done-market-fit-interviews).

If you're about to hire for this work, write down the decision you need to make before you write the job title. Then ask each candidate, psychologist or not, how they would answer it and what result would change their mind. If you'd like an outside view on a decision like that, it's the core of my [consumer psychology consulting](/consumer-psychology).
