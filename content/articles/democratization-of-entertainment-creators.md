The democratization of entertainment is the shift from a few studios, labels and broadcasters deciding what millions of people watch and hear, to anyone being able to make something and find an audience for it. It happened one medium at a time: music, then video, then audio and live streaming. Each time, two things arrived together: making got cheap, and a place to be found appeared.

I'm an applied psychologist, and I'm designing [Pukytoon](/work/pukytoon), a feed of games anyone can make, which is in development. I think games are the last big form of entertainment still waiting for this shift. Here's how it happened to the others, the psychology of why creators hold our attention, and what it costs.

## Who made entertainment in the broadcast era?

For most of the twentieth century, entertainment was scarce at the source. A few studios made films, a few labels signed musicians, a few networks filled the evening. Audiences had real choices, but only among what those few had decided to make.

That model produced extraordinary work. It also meant that talent without access had nowhere to go, and that whole audiences, too small for a network to bother with, were never served at all.

## How did music open up?

Music went first. Home recording put a studio on a laptop, and then came places to be heard. MySpace launched in August 2003 and became a home page for bands. SoundCloud, founded in Stockholm in 2007, launched its site in October 2008, the same year Bandcamp launched to let artists sell directly to fans.

The clearest sign of the shift came later. Lil Nas X's "Old Town Road" went viral on TikTok through the "Yeehaw Challenge," with people making their own videos to it, before it reached No. 1 on the Billboard Hot 100 in April 2019 and stayed there for a then-record 19 weeks. The audience didn't just discover the song. It spread it by making things with it.

## How did video open up?

On April 23, 2005, YouTube co-founder Jawed Karim uploaded the site's first video, "Me at the zoo," a short clip of himself in front of the elephants. Within a few years, ordinary people were making shows from their bedrooms and building audiences that rivaled some of television's.

TikTok pushed the same idea further. ByteDance merged Musical.ly into TikTok on August 2, 2018, bringing together a short-video feed and tools that made creating a response to something else: a sound to use, a trend to join, a video to duet. Few people start from a blank page, which is a big part of why so many people make something.

## How did audio and live streaming open up?

In February 2004 the journalist Ben Hammersley wrote in The Guardian about an "audible revolution" and floated the word podcasting. In June 2005 Apple added podcasts to iTunes, and anyone with a microphone could have a show that listeners subscribed to.

Twitch launched in June 2011 as a gaming spin-off of Justin.tv, and watching people play games live became entertainment in its own right. That detail matters for what comes next: people already love games as something social, something to watch, talk about and share.

## Why do creators hold our attention differently?

Creators don't win attention the way studios did. Three things work in their favor.

The first is closeness. Donald Horton and Richard Wohl (1956) described parasocial interaction as intimacy at a distance: the one-sided sense of knowing someone on a screen. Creators who talk straight to the camera, share their lives and show up on a regular schedule make that intimacy unusually strong.

The second is that the audience can answer back: comments, duets, remixes, reaction videos. Participation turns viewers into a community, and communities return.

The third is that niches can survive. Chris Anderson's "The Long Tail" (Wired, 2004; book, 2006) argued that when shelf space is unlimited, the many small audiences together rival the hits. Kevin Kelly's "1,000 True Fans" (2008) argued that a creator doesn't need millions of fans, only a devoted few willing to support the work directly.

## How each medium opened up

The pattern repeats closely enough to put in a table:

| Medium | Who decided before | What opened it | What made it social |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Music | Labels and radio | Home recording, MySpace, SoundCloud, Bandcamp | Reposts, fans following artists, songs as trends |
| Video | Networks and studios | Phone cameras, YouTube, TikTok | Comments, subscriptions, duets and remixes |
| Audio | Radio stations | Podcast feeds | Subscriptions and a host's voice in your ear |
| Live | Broadcasters | Twitch | Live chat with the person on screen |
| Games | Studios and publishers | AI tools and no-code game makers | Feeds, credit and remixing, still being built |

## What does it cost?

The democratization of entertainment isn't free. Creators burn out under the pressure to post constantly. They depend on algorithms they don't control and can change overnight. Attention, unlike shelf space, is still limited, so a few creators capture much of it, and many make things that almost nobody sees.

The same psychology that makes creators compelling can be turned against audiences, too. Feeds that never end and rewards that never stop are designed to hold people, not to serve them, and I write about where that line falls in [engagement vs. addiction](/insights/engagement-vs-addiction-healthy-engagement). Democratizing who makes things is only half the job; the other half is designing places that are good to spend time in.

## Why are games next?

Games are the last big form of entertainment still made mostly by specialists, because a game is a system, not a recording. Roblox, Minecraft and level editors opened parts of game-making, inside their own worlds. Now AI tools and no-code makers are removing the skills barrier, and the missing piece is the same one every medium needed: a place to be found and a crowd that answers.
