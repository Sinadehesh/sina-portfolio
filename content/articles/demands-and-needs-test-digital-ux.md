The demands-and-needs test feels like a compliance wall because it is so often asked in the insurer's language, at the wrong moment, with answers that then go nowhere. Built as a short structured conversation instead, asking in the customer's words, reflecting the answers back and explaining why the product fits, it becomes the most helpful step in the journey rather than the most resented.

I'm not a lawyer, and I've never worked for an insurer. I'm a consumer psychology consultant who designs funnels and runs research interviews, and the demands-and-needs step is, at heart, a very small interview. That's the lens I'll use here.

## What is the demands-and-needs test?

The EU Insurance Distribution Directive (IDD), Directive (EU) 2016/97, requires that before an insurance contract is concluded, the distributor specifies the customer's demands and needs. For non-life products, the customer also receives a standardized insurance product information document, known as the IPID.

The spirit, as I read it, is simple: find out what this person needs before you sell them something. The letter, meaning what exactly to ask, how to record it and how it interacts with any advice you give, depends on your product, your distribution model and your national rules. This is not legal advice, and nothing below replaces your compliance team.

## Why does it feel like a compliance wall?

In my view, four failure modes explain most of the friction:

1. **The insurer's language.** "Do you require accidental damage cover?" assumes the customer knows what accidental damage cover is.
2. **The wrong moment.** Asked after the price, it reads as a hurdle between the customer and the thing they've already chosen.
3. **Checkbox theater.** A single "I confirm this product meets my needs" box tells the customer the step exists to protect the seller.
4. **Answers that change nothing.** If every path leads to the same product, people sense it and stop answering carefully.

## The Ask-Reflect-Explain loop

The fix is a three-beat loop borrowed from interviewing.

**Ask** about the customer's life, not your product. People can answer "What would you struggle to replace?" far better than "Which cover options do you require?"

**Reflect** the answers back in one line they can correct: "So you mainly want your laptop and bike covered, including outside the home, and you'd rather pay for small losses yourself." This is the moment a form starts to feel like a conversation, and the moment misunderstandings get caught.

**Explain** why the suggestion fits, in a sentence that points back to their words: "That's why we suggest this plan: it covers items away from home, and its higher excess keeps the price down." Then show what it doesn't cover, just as clearly.

## A worked example: rewriting the questions

Imagine a hypothetical home contents journey. The same needs, asked two ways:

| Compliance wording | Customer wording | What the answer changes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| "Do you require accidental damage cover?" | "Is there anything at home you'd struggle to replace if it broke by accident, like a laptop, a TV or glasses?" | Whether to include accidental damage |
| "Do you require cover for possessions outside the home?" | "Do you carry anything valuable when you go out, like a phone, a bike or a camera?" | Whether to add cover for possessions away from home |
| "Select your preferred excess level" | "If something small went wrong, would you rather claim for it, or pay it yourself and keep your price lower?" | The excess level |
| "Do you hold any other insurance policies?" | "Do you already have cover through a bank card, a landlord or an employer?" | Whether to avoid duplicate cover |

Every row passes one rule: if the answer can't change the recommendation, cut the question, or explain why it's needed anyway.

## Designing the demands-and-needs statement

The reflection doesn't have to vanish once the customer clicks on. However your compliance team needs the answers recorded, the customer-facing version deserves design too. I'd make it a short note the customer keeps:

- **Their needs in plain words,** using their own answers wherever possible.
- **The recommendation and the reason,** in one or two sentences.
- **What the policy won't do,** right next to what it will.
- **A way to correct it** before buying, and a copy sent with the policy documents.

A note like that does double duty. It is a record of the sale, and it is the first thing a customer can reread when they wonder, months later, what exactly they bought.

## Can it reduce choice overload?

Insurance ranges are long and hard to compare. Iyengar and Lepper's (2000) jam study made choice overload famous, but a meta-analysis by Scheibehenne, Greifeneder and Todd (2010) found the average effect is close to zero, with important moderators. So I wouldn't promise that fewer options always help. In my reading, though, insurance is the kind of decision where overload is most plausible: the options are hard to compare and most people arrive without a clear preference.

The demands-and-needs answers are the natural filter. Use them to recommend one or two options, each with its reason, and keep the full range one click away. The IPID can help too: because it's a standardized document, it lends itself to a side-by-side comparison on screen rather than a PDF nobody opens. The same logic applies to cover offered inside another purchase, which I look at in [embedded insurance at checkout](/insights/embedded-insurance-checkout-design).

## What interviewing taught me about these questions

I've run a lot of semi-structured interviews: 200 for one research project alone, multi-round user interviews with thematic coding for [RecallMate](/work/recallmate), and market-fit interviews for [Musicity](/work/musicity), where conversations with artists, listeners and venues were coded into a ranked jobs-to-be-done view. Three habits from that work transfer directly:

- **Ask about situations, not preferences.** "Tell me about the last time something broke" gets truer answers than "How important is accidental damage cover to you?" That's the heart of the jobs-to-be-done approach (Christensen, Hall, Dillon & Duncan, 2016), which I describe in [jobs-to-be-done interviews for market fit](/insights/jobs-to-be-done-market-fit-interviews).
- **Never lead.** "You'll want full protection, won't you?" is a sales line dressed as a question. Leading questions make the record look tidy and the recommendation worse.
- **Play it back.** Reflecting an answer is how interviewers check they understood. It works the same way in a form.

## A pre-launch checklist

Before shipping a demands-and-needs step, I'd check that:

- Every question is in the customer's language and can change something.
- The step comes before the price, not after it.
- The customer sees a one-line summary of their needs and can edit it.
- The recommendation refers back to their answers.
- What isn't covered is as visible as what is.
- The full range is available, with no penalty for looking.
- Answers that point away from your product are respected, even when that means no sale.

The irony of the demands-and-needs test is that it asks insurers to do what the best salespeople have always done: listen first. Built as a wall, it adds friction and helps no one. Built as a conversation, it narrows the choice, catches misunderstandings before they turn into claim disputes, and gives customers something quote funnels rarely do, a reason to believe the product was chosen for them.
