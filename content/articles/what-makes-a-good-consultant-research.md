Research on what makes a good consultant, at least in clients' eyes, points to interpersonal skills and knowledge of the client's world rather than similarity. In a review of 17 studies of people choosing a sport psychology consultant, interpersonal skills were consistently among the most important characteristics, while sharing the client's gender, race and age was preferred but consistently ranked least important (Woolway & Harwood, 2020).

That review answers a narrower question than the title, and the gap matters: it's about whom people prefer to hire, not who helps most once the work begins. I read it as a consumer psychology consultant, because choosing a consultant is itself a consumer decision under uncertainty: you're buying something you can't inspect before you pay for it.

## What did Woolway and Harwood's review study?

Woolway and Harwood (2020), both at Loughborough University, published "Consultant characteristics in sport psychology service provision: A critical review and future research directions" in the International Journal of Sport and Exercise Psychology (volume 18, issue 1, pages 46–63; online since April 2018). Despite "critical review" in the title, the method is a systematic narrative review. In May 2017 they searched four databases plus a hand search of journals, and screened 246 records down to 17 studies with 2,962 participants: 2,639 athletes, 115 sport psychologists, 8 coaches and 200 students who weren't athletes. Most studies came from the USA and the UK.

The framing is what makes the review useful outside sport. First impressions and rapport are treated as key to "gaining entry" with a client, the client is treated as a consumer, and the more recent studies use consumer-market methods such as rankings and conjoint analysis, which shows which characteristics drive a choice when several vary at once.

## Which consultant characteristics do clients prefer?

The review covers 12 characteristics: gender, race, athletic background, practitioner title, body build, attire, sport-specific knowledge, interpersonal skills, accreditation, multicultural exposure, physical activity and age. In the authors' summary, the most preferred consultant "was of the same gender, race, and age of the client with a high athletic background, sport-specific knowledge, and interpersonal skills…"

The rankings behind that profile are more useful than the profile itself:

- **Interpersonal skills** were consistently among the most important, and one study ranked them first. The definition used was "the ability… to use his or her personality (e.g., approachable, respectful, caring) to build a positive working relationship", which the authors criticize as too broad. A study of coaches found they valued trustworthiness, energy, personality and confidence (Zakrajsek et al., 2013).
- **Athletic background** ranked second or fourth.
- **Sport-specific knowledge**, being able to "speak your sport's language", was moderately important.
- **Same gender, race and age** were preferred but consistently ranked least important.
- **Accreditation** gave mixed results: first in one study, fifth in another. Attire gave the most mixed results of all, and body build mattered but ranked low.
- **The title** "sport psychologist" carries stigma. After a short educational intervention, it rose from third to first in importance and was preferred over "life coach" and "NLP practitioner" (Woolway & Harwood, 2015).

## What can a consultant control, and what can a client see?

The authors draw two distinctions that I find more useful than any single ranking.

The first is controllability: "Consultant characteristics can be placed on a continuum of controllability", from age, race and gender, which a consultant can't change, to sport knowledge and attire, which they can. The authors conclude that for practitioners, the controllable characteristics matter most.

The second is visibility. Some characteristics are readily visible to a potential client, such as gender, race, attire and body build; others aren't, such as multicultural experience, physical activity and accreditation.

Put together, I see a pattern. In the reviewed studies, the visible cues ranked low or gave mixed results: shared gender and race ranked least important, attire was the most mixed and body build ranked low. My inference for business consulting, which the review didn't test, is that first impressions rest on cues clients themselves rank low, while the controllable signals stay invisible until you ask about them. A good selection process makes them visible.

## Does preferring a consultant mean they're good?

Not necessarily. The review's scope is the decision to seek or hire a consultant, and as Tod, Pullinger and Lafferty (2024) point out, that's a different question from what makes a practitioner helpful once the work has begun. Preference gets a consultant through the door; it isn't evidence of results.

The authors name further limits: 11 of the 17 studies used student samples, participants were mostly prospective clients, and the samples were mostly Western. They also call for research on gatekeepers, such as performance directors. In a company, the gatekeeper might be a founder or procurement, and in my view it pays to know whose criteria a hire really has to meet.

The same caution applies to any research a consultant cites, this review included: a preference measured in one population isn't a law, which is the overgeneralization Pham warns about in [the seven sins of consumer psychology](/insights/seven-sins-of-consumer-psychology-pham).

## Should a consultant explain their title and training?

The review says practitioners should market ethically and "proactively communicate the differences between themselves… and the less regulated… fields of practice", meaning title protection, training routes and methods. In sport, that means explaining how a sport psychologist differs from, say, a life coach.

I take the same advice. In Italy, only people enrolled in Section A of the register (Albo) of the Ordine degli Psicologi may use the title "psicologo" or practise the profession (Law 56/1989; DPR 328/2001), and I'm not registered, so I don't call myself a psychologist. I'm a behavioral scientist and consumer psychology consultant, trained in experimental psychology (M.Sc. in Applied Experimental Psychological Sciences, University of Milano-Bicocca), and my methods are research methods: interviews, experiments and measurement. I'd rather a client hear that from me in the first call than wonder about it later.

This is general information, not legal advice, and rules change, so check with the national professional body.

## How do you choose a consumer psychology consultant?

Here is the scorecard I'd use to choose a consumer psychology, consumer behavior or market research consultant, me included. Every signal is controllable by the consultant and checkable by you; it's my translation of the review's logic to business, not something the review tested. I call it the five-signal scorecard.

| Signal | What good looks like | Question to ask | Red flag |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Knowledge of your market | They ask how your buyers decide before proposing anything. | "What would make our customers hesitate that wouldn't apply in another market?" | The same playbook for every client. |
| Interpersonal skill in the first call | They listen, play your problem back accurately and say what they don't know yet. | "What have you heard so far that you'd want to check?" | A pitch before a single question about your situation. |
| A stated method and measure | They describe the first weeks step by step and name the metric that will judge the work. | "What will you measure, and what result would tell us you were wrong?" | Success defined vaguely, or only after the work is done. |
| Transparency about training and titles | They state their training and title, what they don't do, and why. | "What's your training, and which part of this would you hand to someone else?" | A protected title they can't hold, or credentials you can't check. |
| Relevant work you can check | Past work with their role stated precisely, including what isn't known yet. | "What exactly was your part, and what don't you know about the outcome?" | Big results with no context, or nothing you can verify. |

Use it straight after the first call: mark each signal as clear, unclear or missing before you open the proposal or see the price. In my view, interpersonal skill earns the second meeting; the method and the measure should earn the contract. For what a sound first engagement looks like, see [what a consumer psychology consultant does](/insights/what-does-a-consumer-psychology-consultant-do). If you're unsure which kind of consultant you need, [what a consumer consultant is](/insights/what-is-a-consumer-consultant) separates the jobs that share the name.

## What does this look like in my own work?

The scorecard is the buyer's side of a problem I usually work on from the seller's side. [Optimolds](/work/optimolds), an Italy-based composite engineering and mold-making firm, sells to risk-averse buyers, because a bad mold ruins a production run, and choosing an unfamiliar supplier is the same kind of decision as choosing a consultant. On the website and funnel I designed and built, checkable signals sit next to one clear call to action: reviews, reply time, accepted CAD formats and the fact that the firm is based in Italy. A free 48-hour feasibility check lets buyers see the supplier's judgment on their own part before committing to anything.

For [Musicity](/work/musicity), a music platform, the work was defined by its method and the decision it fed: semi-structured interviews with artists, listeners and venues, coded into a ranked jobs-to-be-done view of demand, then a recommendation on which segment to win first, positioning and launch order. That's the level of specificity I'd ask any research consultant for before hiring them: who they'll talk to, how they'll analyze it and which decision it will feed.

Before your next first call with a consultant, copy the five signals onto a page with a line for each, and fill it in right after the call, before you see the price. If the method-and-measure line is empty, send the question in writing. A consultant who can't say what would prove them wrong is asking you to trust the person, not the work. That includes me: if you're considering my [consumer psychology consulting](/consumer-psychology), score me on the same card.
