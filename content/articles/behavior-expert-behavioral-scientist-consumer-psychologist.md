A behavior expert can be several different professionals, and the one you need depends on whose behavior you want to understand and why. A parent looking for help with a child's development, a dog owner, a ministry that wants taxes paid on time and a company whose customers abandon checkout all need different experts, with different training and different credentials.

I'm one specific kind: a behavioral scientist and consumer psychology consultant in Milan, working on how customers decide, buy and stay. This is the map I'd give a friend: who does what, which credentials to check, and, if you run a business, when you need a consumer behavior expert and what to ask one.

## Which behavior expert do you need?

Start from your question, not from the title. This "who do you need?" table is simplified, and real people often straddle two rows:

| Your question | The expert | Typical training or credential | What they'll do |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| How do we help a person build skills or reduce harmful behavior? | Behavior analyst | BCBA certification from the BACB | Assess the behavior and run a behavior-analytic plan, often in autism and developmental services |
| Why does my dog, cat or horse behave like this? | Applied animal behaviorist or veterinary behaviorist | CAAB or ACAAB (Animal Behavior Society); ACVB board certification for vets | Assess the animal and build a behavior plan |
| Why don't people do what's in their interest, and what would change that? | Behavioral scientist | No single credential: social-science training plus experimental methods | Diagnose the barriers, design a change, test it |
| How do prices, defaults and framing change choices? | Behavioral economist | Economics, with psychology | Model and test choices and incentives |
| Why do customers buy, hesitate, switch or stay? | Consumer behavior expert or consumer psychologist | Consumer psychology, marketing or psychology | Research the decision and change the offer or funnel |
| Can people use our product, and what do they need? | UX researcher | Psychology, human-computer interaction or design research | Interviews, usability tests, experiments |
| A purchase went wrong: a refund, a complaint, a scam | Consumer-rights adviser or journalist, often called a "consumer expert" | Varies by country and service | Explain your rights and help you act on them |

If two rows seem to fit, settle one thing first: are you trying to help one individual, person or animal, or to change what many people do? The first points to the top two rows; the second, to the rest. UX research has its own guide: [what a UX researcher does](/insights/what-does-a-ux-researcher-do).

## Which behavior experts hold a credential?

Some of these roles come with formal credentials, and they're worth checking.

**Behavior analysts:** the credential to look for is the Board Certified Behavior Analyst (BCBA), a graduate-level certification for independent practitioners issued by the Behavior Analyst Certification Board, a nonprofit established in 1998. In general terms it takes a master's degree or higher, behavior-analytic coursework or an accredited program, supervised fieldwork and a pass in the BCBA exam. The requirements change in 2027, so check the board's current rules rather than any summary, including this one.

**Animal behaviorists:** the Animal Behavior Society certifies two levels, the Associate Certified Applied Animal Behaviorist (ACAAB: a master's degree plus two years of professional experience) and the Certified Applied Animal Behaviorist (CAAB: a doctorate in a biological or behavioral science with an animal-behavior emphasis plus five years of experience, or a veterinary degree plus a behavior residency and further experience). Veterinary behaviorists are veterinarians board-certified by the American College of Veterinary Behaviorists.

**Psychologists** are the trickiest case, because "consumer psychologist" names a field while "psychologist" is often a regulated title. In Italy, only psychologists enrolled in Section A of the Albo of the Ordine degli Psicologi may use the title "psicologo" or practice the profession (L. 56/1989; DPR 328/2001). In the UK, "psychologist" alone isn't protected, but nine titles are, such as "registered psychologist" and "clinical psychologist"; using one without being on the HCPC Register is a criminal offense, and "consumer psychologist" isn't among them. In the US, psychologists are licensed by each state, the prevailing standard for independent practice is a doctoral degree in psychology, and state laws vary, so don't assume "consumer psychologist" is free to use there either.

This is general information, not legal advice; rules change, so check with the national body.

## What's the difference between a behavioral scientist and a behavioral expert?

"Behavioral expert" isn't a defined profession. It's how people describe anyone who knows a lot about behavior, so it can mean any row in the table. "Behavioral scientist" is narrower: someone who studies behavior with scientific methods, then designs and tests changes to it, usually across many people, in policy, products or markets. I describe that job step by step in [what a behavioral scientist does](/insights/what-does-a-behavioral-scientist-do).

Two names that often get confused are behavioral scientist and behavior analyst. A behavior analyst typically works with an individual and the people around them, often with a certification such as the BCBA; a behavioral scientist works on decisions at scale, with no single credential. A behavioral economist is a close cousin who starts from economics: predictable departures from "rational" choice, such as loss aversion (Kahneman & Tversky, 1979) and the pull of defaults (Johnson & Goldstein, 2003).

## What does a consumer behavior expert do, and when do you need one?

A consumer behavior expert works on why customers buy, hesitate, switch or stay, and turns the answer into a business decision: which customers to serve first, what to offer, how to price it, where the funnel loses people. The work combines behavioral data for where people stop, interviews for why, and experiments to check that a change works. Consumer psychologists cover the mental side of the same question, and I explain their role in [what a consumer psychologist does](/insights/what-does-a-consumer-psychologist-do).

Two near-namesakes do different jobs. "Consumer expert" in the media often means a consumer-rights journalist or advocate who helps people with complaints, refunds and scams, and some consulting practices specialize in consumer-goods and retail companies, where the expertise is the industry rather than the behavior.

A company usually needs a consumer behavior expert when:

- you can see where customers drop off, but nobody can say why;
- customers praise the idea in interviews and then don't buy;
- you have to choose which segment to win first;
- a change to a price, plan or offer is being argued opinion against opinion.

And when it doesn't: if hardly anyone visits, you have a reach problem, and if the product doesn't do what it promises, fix the product first.

## What should you ask a behavior expert before you hire one?

I call this the five-question screen. Each question comes with what a good answer sounds like:

1. **"Which behavior, exactly, will you study?"** A good answer names one behavior and how it will be measured. "Your customers' mindset" is not an answer.
2. **"Which method will you start with, and why?"** Data shows where, interviews show why, and an experiment shows cause. The method should fit the question.
3. **"What are you trained and allowed to do, and what not?"** Good experts name their limits plainly, including titles they don't hold.
4. **"What did your research change last time?"** Look for a decision that changed, not a deck that was delivered.
5. **"What result would change your recommendation?"** If the answer is "none", you're buying an opinion.

Before you contact any behavior expert, write your question as one sentence that names a group of people, a behavior and a setting: "first-time buyers abandon our quote form at the upload step", not "we need better conversion". That sentence tells you which row of the table you're in, and it's the first thing a good expert will ask you for anyway.
