The social side of making games is what turns a tool into a medium. Once anyone can make a game in minutes, the point stops being the game alone and becomes the people around it: who made it, who it's for, and who answers it. A game made in ten minutes for one friend can matter more than a polished game made for no one in particular.

I'm an applied psychologist, and I'm designing [Pukytoon](/work/pukytoon), a feed of games anyone can make, which is still in development. [The democratization of game development](/insights/democratization-of-game-development) is usually told as a story about tools. I think the social side decides whether it happens, so here it is piece by piece: what changes between people when everyone can make games, and what that asks of the design.

## Why do people make games for each other?

Think about why people make each other birthday cards, playlists or short videos. It's rarely for the craft. It's to say something to someone: I thought of you, I know you, this is our joke.

Games can carry those messages now. A runner where your friend is the hero. A quiz only your class can pass. A game about the café where you met. None of these needs to be polished, because their value comes from who made them and who they're for. An audience of one is a real audience.

Self-determination theory names three needs that make an activity feel worth doing: autonomy, competence and relatedness (Ryan & Deci, 2000). Making a game for someone meets all three, and relatedness most directly. That's the psychological core of the social side: making becomes a way of being with people.

## How do players become makers?

In almost every online community, most people only watch. Jakob Nielsen (2006) summed this up as the 90-9-1 rule: most people never contribute, a few contribute now and then, and a tiny group makes most of what everyone sees. It's a rule of thumb rather than a law, but the shape shows up almost everywhere.

The answer isn't to push the watchers into making. It's to build a ladder where every rung is a small step from the one below, so people climb when they're ready. I call it the maker's ladder:

| Rung | What the person does | What the design must make easy | In Pukytoon |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1. Play | Tries a game | Trying costs nothing | A feed with one game per screen, one swipe to the next |
| 2. React | Shows they liked it | A response that takes one tap | Likes |
| 3. Pass it on | Sends it to someone | One link that opens anywhere | A share link for every game |
| 4. Remix | Changes someone's game | Starting from a game that already works | Make your own version of any shared Studio game |
| 5. Make | Makes a new game | Choosing and tuning instead of coding | The Creator Studio, or pasting a Claude, Gemini or Kimi link |
| 6. Be followed | Makes for people who are waiting | A home for a maker's games, and an audience | Following makers, coming next |

In my view the rung that matters most is the fourth. Going from playing straight to a blank page is a jump. Changing one thing in a game you just enjoyed is a step.

## Why is remixing so important?

Lawrence Lessig (2008) described two kinds of culture: read-only, where people consume what professionals make, and read/write, where they answer it by making something of their own. For most of their history, games have been read-only. Remixing makes them read/write.

A remix is a reply. When you make your own version of someone's game, you're saying "I liked this, and here's mine." A chain of versions reads like a conversation, each maker answering the last.

Remixing is also a way in. Mitchel Resnick and colleagues (2009) set out to make Scratch more social than the tools before it, and its online community lets anyone share a project and remix someone else's. Seeing how another person's game works, then changing it, is a natural way to learn. There's a motivational reason too: people are more likely to finish something when they start with some progress already made, the endowed progress effect (Nunes & Drèze, 2006). A remix starts with a working game, so the first step is a change, not an empty screen.

In Pukytoon, any shared Studio game can be remixed with Make your own version. A Studio game is a few hundred bytes of settings and rules rather than code, so remixing it is safe.

## Why does credit matter so much?

If remixing is the conversation, credit is the courtesy that keeps it going. Andrés Monroy-Hernández and colleagues (2011) studied remixing in the Scratch community and found a telling difference. When remixers credited the original maker in their own words, reactions were friendlier. When the system added credit automatically, it didn't have the same effect. The young makers they interviewed saw a difference between attribution, which a computer can add, and credit, which comes from a person and carries thanks.

My takeaway as a designer has two parts. Show the maker on every game by default: in Pukytoon every shared game carries its maker's name and @handle. And don't stop at automatic labels; make it easy to say thanks in your own words at the moment you make your own version of someone's game.

Credit also builds status. Reciprocity is one of the strongest social norms (Cialdini, 1984), and in a community of makers, being remixed with thanks is a kind of reputation that nobody can buy.

## Why do makers need followers?

Making a second game is harder than making the first. The first comes from curiosity; the second needs a reason, and the strongest reason is someone waiting for it.

Followers turn a maker's games into a body of work and a maker into someone people know. Likes do part of this job already: they tell a maker that someone played and enjoyed their game, which is feedback on competence, and they help players choose what to try, which is social proof (Cialdini, 1984). Following makers is the next step I'm designing for Pukytoon, so that each new game reaches the people who enjoyed the last one.

Audiences have a dark side. Popularity feeds itself: what's liked gets shown and what's shown gets liked, so newcomers can disappear. I think a fair feed has to give new makers a first audience on purpose.

Rewards need care too. When people make things for the joy of it, paying them in points for publishing can turn play into work, the overjustification effect (Lepper, Greene & Nisbett, 1973) that I cover in [gamification that doesn't backfire](/insights/gamification-overjustification-edtech). A large meta-analysis found that positive, informational feedback tends to support intrinsic motivation, while expected tangible rewards tend to undermine it (Deci, Koestner & Ryan, 1999). "Someone played your game and liked it" is the first kind.

## How do people play together, even apart?

Most social play around small games happens at different times. You send a game, and your friend plays it on the bus an hour later. One link per game means a game can travel through the group chats people already use, the way they pass around a song or a meme.

That matters more than it sounds. A game sent to a group becomes a shared reference: everyone has played the same thing, and there's something to talk about. A game from a stranger in the feed can become a game you send to a friend, which is where a feed and a friendship meet. And playing a game a friend made is a way of spending time with them when you can't be in the same place.

## What makes a community of makers last?

Henry Jenkins and colleagues (2006) described participatory culture as one with low barriers to artistic expression, strong support for creating and sharing, informal mentorship in which experienced members help newcomers, members who believe their contributions matter, and some social connection between them. I read it as a design brief:

| Condition | What it means for a game platform | In Pukytoon |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Low barriers to making | A first game in minutes, without code | The Creator Studio, and pasting a link |
| Support for sharing | Sharing is one step, and the work gets seen | Sign-in with just an email, a shared feed, one link per game |
| Informal mentorship | Newcomers learn from experienced makers | Make your own version starts you from someone's game; community features come next |
| Contributions matter | Makers can see that people played and liked their work | Likes, and the maker's name and @handle on every game |
| Social connection | People know each other and come back for each other | Following makers, coming next |

The last two rows are the hardest, and they're where I'm spending the most design time.

## Who gets to make games, and why does it matter?

When only specialists make games, games reflect a narrow set of people, places and tastes. When anyone can make one, there can be games in every language, about every town, school and family. A teacher could make a game for one lesson, a grandparent one for a grandchild, a local shop one for its regulars. These are hypothetical, but they're the kind of small, specific games that no studio will ever make, because their audience is a few people.

This is the part of democratization I care about most. It isn't about more games. It's about different people getting to say things with games.

## How do you keep a community of makers safe?

Openness brings the worst along with the best: spam, copies without credit, unsafe code and cruelty. A community stays open to beginners only if beginners trust it, so safety is a social feature, not an extra.

In Pukytoon every pasted game runs sealed off from the app and from the other games, and people can report and hide what they don't want to see. Until the safety checks for shared code are ready, only Studio games and links to Claude, Gemini or Kimi can be shared with everyone.

## What Pukytoon has today, and what comes next

In the version I'm building today: a swipe feed of games, the Creator Studio, adding a game by pasting a Claude, Gemini or Kimi link or its code, sign-in with just an email, sharing with everyone, likes, one link per game, Make your own version, the maker's name and @handle on every shared game, and reports and hiding. Next: following makers, community features and an asset library.
