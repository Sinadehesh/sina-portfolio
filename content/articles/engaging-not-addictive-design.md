I design apps and games to be very engaging, and I work just as hard to make sure they aren't addictive. The difference is simple to say and hard to build: people should come back because something adds to their day, and they should be able to leave whenever they like, at no cost. Engagement earns each return; addiction makes leaving feel expensive.

I'm an applied psychologist, and I write about where that line falls in [engagement vs. addiction](/insights/engagement-vs-addiction-healthy-engagement). This article is about my own work: the rules I hold my products to, how they measure up, and the mechanics I watch most closely.

## My rules for engagement without addiction

Seven rules guide how I design for engagement. Each one exists because of a specific way products slide from engaging into compulsive.

1. Nothing random should ever be sold for real money. Chance can be part of play; it shouldn't be part of a purchase. Variable-ratio rewards produce some of the highest and most persistent response rates of any reinforcement schedule (Ferster & Skinner, 1957), and putting a price on them is how play turns into gambling. In Zombie Bomber the rule is written into the code: nothing random is ever sold.
2. Odds are honest. If there's a wheel, every slice has the same chance.
3. Ads never break play. Rewarded ads are always the player's choice, and full-screen ads only appear between levels, never during one.
4. Things end. Stories have endings, programs have a last day, and a session has a natural place to stop.
5. Leaving costs nothing. Taking a break shouldn't wipe out progress, and the app shouldn't punish you for having a life.
6. There's always a way out. Where a product adds friction, there's an escape hatch, because people who feel trapped resent the product and quit it.
7. Making beats consuming. Where I can, I point engagement toward making something, not just taking it in.

## How the rules show up in my products

Here's how they show up in products I've designed, taken from how each one is built:

| Product | What brings people back | The guardrail |
| --- | --- | --- |
| [Gem Jar](/work/gem-jar) | Quests every 8 hours, a Daily Challenge, a Daily Gift, levels and gem sets to unlock | The Lucky Wheel gives one free spin a day and every slice has the same chance; extra spins come only from ads the player chooses to watch, and an ad never breaks a run |
| [Zombie Bomber](/work/zombie-bomber) | 300 levels, an upgrade shop and bigger bombs to merge | Nothing random is ever sold; full-screen ads only between levels, never before level 4 and never during a level; on the website there are no ads and nothing to buy |
| [The Loop](/work/the-loop) | A story about AI and work, and 23 endings to find | It's a story: it ends, and each ending is a natural place to stop |

## The mechanics I watch most closely

Not every mechanic I use is risk-free, and I'd rather be clear about the ones I watch.

Streaks are first. [OopsCupid](/work/oopscupid) uses daily challenges and streaks, and Gem Jar's Daily Gift grows each day in a row. Streaks work partly through loss aversion: losses loom larger than equivalent gains (Kahneman & Tversky, 1979), so people try hard not to lose a streak. That can turn a pleasant habit into a duty. A small, gentle streak is usually fine; a streak that wipes out a lot of progress after one missed day is a warning sign. In DeBubble I left streaks out entirely, because self-care shouldn't come with a penalty.

The Lucky Wheel is second. It's a variable reward by design, which is why it's free, limited to one free spin a day, never sold and built with equal odds. A wheel that can be bought, or that slows down next to the jackpot, would break my rules.

The feed is third. A feed is a powerful format, and I've designed [Costly](/work/costly), an app that puts a real-money price on doomscrolling, precisely because feeds can take more time than people mean to give. In Pukytoon, a game doesn't play at you; you tap to start it, and the point of the feed is to move people from playing toward making their own games.

## Why I built a slot machine I'd never sell

[Dopamine Box](/work/dopamine-box) is a behavioral experiment on betting. It runs on a mock-token "spin to claim" loop, with variable rewards, near-misses and scarcity tags, and no real money involved. I built it to observe how people bet compared with the Kelly criterion, the mathematically optimal stake, and which attitudes and biases pull them off course. I explain the mechanics in [variable rewards and near-misses](/insights/variable-rewards-near-miss-ethics).

Building those mechanics taught me how little effort each one takes to add, and how deliberately each one works on attention. That's exactly why they stay inside a research prototype with mock tokens, and out of the games I put in front of players.

## Engagement I'm proud of

The engagement I want comes from the play itself. In Gem Jar, tuning the gravity, making the more precious gems heavier and changing the sound effects made play much more satisfying; nothing about that change makes it harder to stop. In one of my games, moving ads out of the flow of play made playing time go up dramatically. Players stayed because the game stopped interrupting them, not because it found a new way to hold them.

That's the kind of number I'm happy to push up. If a change raises time spent by making the experience better, it's engagement. If it raises time spent by making leaving harder, it's the other thing, and it doesn't belong in a product I'd stand behind.

## How you can judge for yourself

You don't have to take my word for it. Ask of any app, mine included: Would you still come back if it stopped sending reminders? Does it end? Can you leave without losing something you care about? Is anything random sold for money? Do you feel better after using it than before? The full version of that test, with the research behind it, is in [engagement vs. addiction](/insights/engagement-vs-addiction-healthy-engagement).

If a product fails those questions, the fix is almost always in the design. That's good news for designers, because it means the line between engaging and addictive isn't luck. It's a choice we make in every feature.
