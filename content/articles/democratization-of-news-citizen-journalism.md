The democratization of news is the long shift from a few printers and editors deciding what the public learns to anyone with a phone being able to report what they see. It came in steps: printed news sheets in the 1600s, the penny press in the 1830s, and then blogs, phones and social feeds that turned witnesses into reporters. Each step widened who could tell the news, and each one changed what it takes to trust it.

I'm an applied psychologist, and much of my work is about how people decide what to trust. It's the same question I ask when I design products, including [Pukytoon](/work/pukytoon), a feed of games anyone can make. Here's how news opened up, what we gained and lost, and the psychology that decides what spreads.

## How did news begin?

In 1605 Johann Carolus, a Strasbourg publisher, petitioned the city council about his printed weekly, the Relation aller Fürnemmen und gedenckwürdigen Historien, which is widely recognized as the first newspaper; its earliest surviving issues date from 1609. It was part of the long story I describe in [from Gutenberg to blogging](/insights/gutenberg-to-blogging-collective-knowledge): once printing was cheap, news could travel on paper instead of by word of mouth.

Newspapers were long priced for the few. Then, on September 3, 1833, Benjamin Day's New York Sun went on sale for a penny, and the penny press made the daily paper something ordinary people bought every day.

## Who decided what was news?

For most of the twentieth century, a small number of editors and broadcasters decided what the public would learn. The psychologist Kurt Lewin introduced the idea of a "gatekeeper" in the 1940s, and in 1950 David Manning White studied a newspaper wire editor he called "Mr. Gates" as he chose which stories to print and which to throw away.

Gatekeepers brought standards: checking facts, naming sources, correcting errors. They also brought blind spots. Whatever didn't pass their gate, because it seemed unimportant, came from the wrong people or happened in the wrong place, never reached the public at all.

## How did the web open the gate?

The web removed the gate. Bloggers could publish without an editor, and in 2004 the journalist Dan Gillmor called it grassroots journalism in We the Media. In 2006 Jay Rosen wrote about "the people formerly known as the audience," who no longer just received the news but made it.

Then phones put a camera in every witness's pocket. On January 15, 2009, when US Airways Flight 1549 landed on the Hudson River, Janis Krums, aboard a ferry that went to help, posted one of the first images of the plane, standing on the water with passengers on its wings, before television cameras arrived. The person who saw it first was now the person who reported it first.

## What did we gain, and what did we lose?

Both models have real strengths, and each one's strength is the other's weakness:

| | Gatekept news | Open news |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Who reports | Professional journalists | Anyone who's there |
| Speed | Hours to a day | Seconds |
| Range of voices | Narrow | Wide |
| Checking | Before publishing | After publishing, if at all |
| What spreads | What editors chose | What people share |
| Accountability | A named outlet | Often nobody |

We gained speed, reach and voices that the old gate kept out. We lost the default of checking before publishing, and with it a shared sense of what had been verified.

## Why does false news spread so fast?

The weakness of open news is psychological, not technical. Soroush Vosoughi, Deb Roy and Sinan Aral (2018) studied news stories shared on Twitter and found that false news spread farther, faster, deeper and more broadly than the truth. False stories were more novel and inspired fear, disgust and surprise. Bots spread true and false news at the same rate, so the difference came from people.

Repetition adds to it. Lynn Hasher, David Goldstein and Thomas Toppino (1977) found that statements people had heard before were judged more likely to be true, an effect now known as the illusory truth effect. In a feed, the same claim can reach you many times in an hour, from different people, and each repetition makes it feel a little more familiar and a little more true.

The good news is that small nudges help. Gordon Pennycook and colleagues (2021) found that shifting people's attention to accuracy, for example by asking them to judge whether a single headline was true, improved the quality of the news they shared afterward. Most people don't want to spread falsehoods. In the moment they tap share, they just aren't thinking about truth.

## How should open news be designed?

If the problem lives in the moment of sharing, so does much of the fix. These are the five design choices I'd make for any product where news spreads, drawing on my work on [positive friction](/insights/positive-friction-ux-design):

1. Slow the share, not the read. A moment of friction before resharing, such as a prompt to open the article first, costs a reader a second and costs a rumor its momentum.
2. Show where and when. Put the original source and the time a claim first appeared next to it.
3. Credit the witness. The person who saw it first should be named, and stay named as it spreads.
4. Ask about accuracy. A light question about whether something is true shifts attention to truth at the right moment.
5. Make corrections travel. A correction should be as easy to share as the claim it corrects, and should reach the people who saw the original.

## Can news be played?

There's one more step in this story, and it's the one I'm working on. Ian Bogost, Simon Ferrari and Bobby Schweizer (2010) described newsgames: games about current events, or games that let you try the system behind a story. Reading that a policy affects rent is one thing; playing a month on that rent is another.

My story game [The Loop](/work/the-loop) isn't a newsgame, but it works the same way: it lets you feel, one reasonable decision at a time, how people end up rubber-stamping what AI decides. As making games becomes as easy as making videos, I expect citizens to make games about their own towns and their own news. That's part of why I'm building Pukytoon, which is still in development; if you'd like to know more, or to fund it, [email me](mailto:sinadehesh@gmail.com?subject=About%20Pukytoon).

The democratization of news gave everyone a voice. The work now is giving everyone, readers and platforms alike, the habits and the design to be heard accurately.
